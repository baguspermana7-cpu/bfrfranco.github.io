OVHcloud SBG2 Strasbourg Data-Center Fire (10 March 2021)

OVHcloud 2021-03-10 9h 27m core impact FirePower

In the early hours of 10 March 2021 a fire broke out in a ground-floor energy room at OVHcloud's SBG2 facility in Strasbourg, where the site's UPS units and their lead-acid battery banks were housed. Video surveillance and the fire panel traced the initiating event to a near-simultaneous electrical fault on UPS2 and its connected batteries. With no automatic suppression system in any of the five campus buildings, and a free-cooling design that used intumescent-treated raw-wood floors and single-skin cladding to aid airflow, the fire spread through the entire structure. De-energisation was slow because there was no accessible general site cut-off, forcing the grid operator to isolate an upstream substation; current was still present in the burning building well after the cut order. SBG2 was destroyed in its entirety, four of twelve rooms in adjacent SBG1 were partially destroyed, and the SBG2–SBG3 inter-building link was impacted. Roughly 30,000 servers were lost and about 3.6 million websites went offline. There were no casualties or injuries. French investigator BEA-RI could establish the fault location but not its precise cause, leaving several hypotheses open pending a court-expert examination.

Root cause

The fire originated on the ground floor of SBG2 in the energy room complex housing the UPS units and their lead-acid battery banks. Video surveillance and the site fire-control panel establish an electrical fault at UPS2 and at the batteries connected to it (the two were in separate rooms), with fire outbreaks appearing almost simultaneously on the batteries and on an inverter. The precise cause of the UPS failure could not be established by BEA-RI; candidate hypotheses include liquid or humidity from the nearby cooling system, a fault linked to a maintenance operation performed the same morning, or operation of the inverter outside its normal ranges. The ambiguity is deepened by the use of lead-acid (not lithium) batteries, which BEA-RI characterises as more robust and stable, with internal short circuits being rare. The precise ignition mechanism was left to an ongoing court-expert examination.

Contributing factors

  • No automatic fire suppression system in any of the five Strasbourg buildings — water could not be applied very early without first waiting for power interruption.
  • Free-cooling building design optimised for airflow and low cooling energy: intumescent-treated raw-wood floors and single-skin/aluminium-strip cladding promoted rapid vertical fire spread; emergency services judged early the design would not allow containment.
  • No easily accessible general site cut-off device, forcing de-energisation at an upstream source substation — current remained present in the fully-ablaze SBG2 at 02:14, long after the 00:58 cut order, delaying massive water deployment.
  • Inadequate on-site water resources: a single public hydrant delivering <60 m3/h, no OVH extinguishing-water reserve, and no means to pump from the Rhine canal, forcing reliance on the EUROPA fireboat.
  • UPS and its connected batteries located in separate rooms, so protecting the battery room alone would not have prevented ignition originating across the charging chain.
  • Initial ICPE water-resource requirements were not met at the site (though BEA-RI judges compliance alone would likely not have prevented this specific fire given its kinetics).

Correction of errors (COE)

  • Redefine the regulated unit as a 'charging workshop' — the room housing the batteries plus the charging equipment directly connected to them — so building fire-behaviour requirements cover the whole chain (protecting the battery storage room alone would not have prevented this fire). DGPR (French regulator) · Recommended
  • In rebuilding and future data centers, incorporate SBG2 feedback into the design of buildings and energy (F/G) rooms, into detection and firefighting means, and into emergency procedures. OVHcloud · Recommended
  • Establish and drill, through exercises, emergency procedures for securing electrical installations at the Strasbourg site to facilitate public emergency-service intervention (directly addressing the de-energisation delay). OVHcloud · Recommended
  • Carry out a fleet-wide audit of all OVH facilities to study each site's vulnerability to fire risk. OVHcloud · Recommended
  • Follow the battery manufacturer's guidance and set up monitoring of battery operating conditions and state to define preventive-maintenance/replacement rules; where battery counts are large, deploy BMS/polling data-management (VII.5). OVHcloud · Recommended

Lessons learnt

  • Fire detection without automatic suppression buys almost no containment time in a data center — SBG2 rooms had detection but no extinguishing system, and water could not be applied early without first cutting power.
  • The battery-charging chain, not just the battery room, is the true hazard unit; the UPS and its batteries were in separate rooms and the fault spanned both, so single-room protection would not have prevented the fire.
  • Energy-efficient free-cooling construction (combustible raw-wood floors, single-skin cladding) trades fire compartmentation for airflow — the same design that cuts cooling energy accelerated vertical fire spread.
  • A facility must be de-energisable quickly and locally; relying on an upstream substation cut left the building live for over an hour, blocking safe massive water application while the fire grew.
  • On-site water resilience matters — a single hydrant below 60 m3/h with no reserve and no canal-pumping capability forced dependence on an external fireboat that arrived roughly two hours into the incident.
  • Preventive battery monitoring (operating conditions and state, per manufacturer rules, with BMS at scale) is essential observability; the exact fault cause could not be determined afterward.
  • A total-loss fire is a plausible, not far-fetched, scenario for a data center and must be planned for by both operators and local emergency services.

Improvements & remediation

  • Rebuild energy rooms and buildings with automatic fire suppression and proper fire-behaviour construction, incorporating SBG2 feedback into detection, firefighting means, and emergency procedures.
  • Install an easily accessible general site cut-off and drill electrical-securing procedures so responders can safely deploy water within minutes rather than hours.
  • Provide on-site extinguishing-water reserves and/or canal-pumping capability sized for a widespread-fire scenario, and ensure ICPE water-resource requirements are met.
  • Treat the whole charging workshop (batteries plus charging equipment) as the regulated fire unit and design its compartmentation accordingly.
  • Deploy battery monitoring / BMS with manufacturer-based preventive-maintenance and replacement rules across large battery fleets.
  • Run a fleet-wide fire-vulnerability audit of all facilities and feed findings into rebuild standards.
  • Advance professional fire-safety guides and ANSSI certification for data centers instead of ICPE classification, reflecting that the dominant consequence is economic/strategic (data loss and service unavailability) rather than human or environmental.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism: an undetermined fault in the power backbone

The incident began not in the IT halls but in SBG2's power backbone. Surveillance and the fire panel place the initiating electrical fault on UPS2 and its associated lead-acid battery bank, with outbreaks appearing almost simultaneously on batteries and an inverter located in a separate room. Crucially, BEA-RI could not establish why the UPS failed, floating three hypotheses — liquid or humidity from the adjacent cooling system, a fault tied to a maintenance operation performed that same morning, or operation of the inverter outside normal ranges. The use of lead-acid rather than lithium chemistry, which BEA-RI regards as robust and rarely subject to internal short circuits, makes the ignition all the more anomalous and is why the precise mechanism was handed to a court-appointed expert.

Why it cascaded: a design optimised against containment

Several deliberate design choices converted a localised electrical fault into a total loss. No automatic suppression existed in any of the five buildings, so water could not be applied until power was cut. The free-cooling architecture — intumescent-treated raw-wood floors and single-skin/aluminium cladding chosen to maximise heat exchange and minimise cooling energy — offered combustible pathways and little compartmentation, and responders judged on arrival that it would not permit containment. Compounding this, there was no accessible general cut-off: de-energisation had to wait for an upstream substation isolation, and current was still present in the fully-ablaze building at 02:14, more than an hour after the 00:58 cut order. Each efficiency- or cost-driven decision individually plausible combined into a system with no early line of defence.

Recovery: external water, mass replacement, and cross-site failover

With one hydrant under 60 m3/h and no on-site reserve or canal-pumping capability, effective firefighting depended on the EUROPA fireboat, which arrived around 02:57, and on full de-energisation at 03:28; the fire was controlled by 06:45 and extinguished by 10:02, with the intervention closed at 18:13 and no casualties. Service recovery leaned on the surviving campus and OVH's manufacturing: SBG3 and SBG4 restarted from 17 March, the Croix factory doubled output from 1,500 to over 3,000 servers per week, and by 23 April Bare Metal, Public Cloud and VPS were roughly 98–99% re-delivered while Hosted Private Cloud reached 88% on other sites, with 8,000 services slated to return on their original IPs. Customers who suffered permanent data loss, however, could not be made whole by capacity alone.

Organisational and systemic factors

The investigation frames the loss less as a freak accident than as an accumulation of risk-acceptance decisions: choosing no suppression across the whole campus, building for cooling efficiency over fire compartmentation, and not meeting the initial ICPE water-resource requirements. BEA-RI's remedies are structural — redefine the regulated 'charging workshop' to cover batteries plus charging equipment, drill electrical-securing procedures, audit the entire fleet for fire vulnerability, and monitor battery state proactively. Notably, BEA-RI concluded data centers should not be ICPE-classified, arguing the dominant consequence of such a fire is economic and strategic (data loss and service unavailability) rather than human or environmental, and pointing instead to professional fire-safety guides and ANSSI certification.

Cross-incident pattern: single-facility concentration and thin defence-in-depth

SBG2 is a canonical example of correlated failure: tens of thousands of servers and millions of dependent websites concentrated in one building whose defensive layers — suppression, compartmentation, rapid de-energisation, and water supply — were all thin or absent simultaneously, so a single power-room fault removed everything at once. The recurring lesson mirrors other large outages: efficiency and cost optimisations that quietly erode independent safety layers, plus customer over-reliance on a single site without geo-redundant backups, turn a contained component fault into a headline loss. Resilience here was ultimately proven not by the facility's own defences but by cross-site failover and industrial replacement capacity.

Technical deep-dive

The initiating event sits in the UPS/battery topology of SBG2's ground-floor energy rooms. BEA-RI's forensic basis is the site's own video surveillance and fire-control panel telemetry, which localise an electrical fault to UPS2 and to the lead-acid battery string connected to it — with the UPS and its batteries deliberately sited in separate rooms. Fire outbreaks registered almost simultaneously on the batteries and on an inverter, indicating the fault propagated across the charging chain rather than being confined to a single component. SBG2's battery population was substantial: 800 lead-acid units of roughly 30 kg and 13 kW unit power each. Because lead-acid chemistry is comparatively stable and internal shorts are rare, BEA-RI could not close out the ignition cause and enumerated three non-exclusive hypotheses — ingress of liquid/humidity from the physically adjacent cooling system, a defect introduced by the maintenance operation performed the same morning, or operation of the inverter outside its qualified envelope — deferring the definitive determination to a court-expert. The decisive engineering weakness was the response envelope, not merely the fault. With detection but no automatic suppression, and no local general cut-off, the facility could not be made electrically safe fast enough to permit water: the cut order at 00:58 propagated only to the upstream SER (Strasbourg Électricité Réseaux) source substation effective at 01:50, yet 02:14 telemetry showed SBG2 still energised and fire spreading to SBG1, and full de-energisation was achieved only at 03:28. In parallel, the water side was undersized — one hydrant below 60 m3/h, no on-site reserve, and no Rhine-canal pumping means — so effective flow depended on the fireboat. The building fabric closed the loop: intumescent-treated raw-wood floors and single-skin/aluminium cladding, engineered for free-cooling heat exchange, provided combustible vertical pathways and negligible compartmentation. The net systemic failure mode is the coincidence of a low-probability power-electronics fault with an environment offering no independent means of early suppression, rapid isolation, or adequate water — the hallmark of insufficient defence-in-depth around the energy plant.
