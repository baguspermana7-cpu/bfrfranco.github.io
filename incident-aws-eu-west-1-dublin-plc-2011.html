AWS EU West (Dublin) 2011: Transformer Loss and a Generator PLC Halt

Amazon Web Services 2011-08-07 3d 10h to substantial restoration Power
SummaryTimelineResponseFMEACausesActionsSources

Summary

On Sunday 7 August 2011 a utility transformer feeding much of one AWS EU West zone in Dublin failed. A PLC that brings the backup generators onto the bus halted, the UPS drained and the zone went dark. Staff synchronised generators by hand and restored the network by 13:49 PDT, but EBS recovery took until 10 August.

Users affected
Almost all EC2 instances and 58% of EBS volumes in one EU West zone; Single-AZ RDS in that zone; regional EC2 launch errors for over an hour.
Duration
3d 10h — Trigger 10:41 PDT 7 Aug → 98% of recovery snapshots delivered 20:25 PDT 10 Aug (3 d 9 h 44 min)
Financial
Not disclosed; AWS gave a 10-day 100% credit to customers in the zone
Scope
Single Availability Zone, with regional API impact
Site
AWS EU West Availability Zone (facility not named) · Dublin, Ireland, eu-west-1
Services down
Amazon EC2, Amazon EBS, Amazon RDS, EC2 APIs (EU West)

Root cause and contributing factors

TRIGGER: at 10:41 PDT on Sunday 7 August 2011 the utility's 110 kV 10 MW transformer serving a large part of one AWS EU West Availability Zone in Dublin failed. The utility first blamed lightning and later withdrew that; the cause of the transformer failure is not given. POWER CHAIN: the generators started, but one of the PLCs that synchronise their phase before closing them onto the bus did not complete the connection of a portion of them.
Full root-cause narrative
AWS believes a large ground fault detected by the PLC made it stop. With no utility and too few generators online, the UPS batteries drained and almost all EC2 instances and 58% of EBS volumes in the zone lost power, together with the network gear that linked the zone to the internet and the other zones. Staff synchronised generators by hand from 11:54; network connectivity was back at 13:49. RECOVERY CHAIN: the regional EC2 APIs suffered because management servers kept routing to the dead zone and queued launches for it. EBS re-mirroring exhausted spare capacity, so servers were trucked in at night. Volumes that lost every replica had to be rebuilt as recovery snapshots through S3, which took until 10 August. LATENT ROOT: a single control device stood between a working generator fleet and the load, and the storage recovery design assumed far fewer simultaneous failures than a full zone power loss produces.

Contributing factors by category

  • The utility's 110 kV 10 MW transformer failed; the utility first cited lightning, then said it was not lightning (AWS summary; DCK).
  • A generator-synchronising PLC did not complete connecting part of the generators, believed to be due to a large ground fault (AWS summary).
  • UPS batteries gave only a short ride-through and drained before generators could be synchronised by hand (AWS summary).
  • The zone's network gear lost power too, so instances that regained power stayed unreachable until 13:49 (AWS summary).
  • EC2 management servers kept routing to the dead zone and queued launches for it, overloading the regional API (AWS summary).
  • Spare EBS capacity was too small for re-mirroring at this scale, and night-time logistics delayed extra servers (AWS summary).
  • Volumes that lost every replica needed recovery snapshots through S3, a slow process for large volumes (AWS summary).
  • A DNS connectivity issue and a software bug delayed failover of some RDS Multi-AZ databases (AWS summary).

Analysis

One controller between the generators and the load

The generators started; the PLC that brings them onto the bus did not finish. That turned a routine utility loss into a zone outage. AWS's answer, a cold isolated backup PLC, accepts that the controller is itself a single point of failure.

Power returned before service did

Generators were online at 11:54 but most network gear stayed dark until 13:49, so powered instances could not be reached. Restoration order matters as much as restoration.

A zone failure that leaked into the region

Management servers in healthy zones kept sending and queuing work for the dead zone, so launches slowed across EU West until AWS removed the failed servers at 12:00.

The three-day tail

EBS spare capacity ran out during re-mirroring, and volumes that lost all replicas had to be rebuilt via S3. Recovery snapshots reached 98% only on the evening of 10 August.

A second, unrelated failure

In the same week an EBS snapshot-cleanup bug deleted blocks still referenced by some snapshots. AWS paused that recovery to fight the power event, then finished it on 8 August.

Dating the event

The summary says only 'August 7th' and 'Sunday'. The AWS summaries index lists it under 2014, but 7 August was a Sunday in 2011, not 2014, and Data Center Knowledge reported the outage on 7 August 2011.

Technical deep-dive
Paralleling switchgear lets several generators share a bus only once their voltage, frequency and phase match; a PLC decides when to close each breaker. If that PLC halts, perhaps on a ground-fault input as AWS believes, healthy generators run with nowhere to deliver power. Manual synchronisation works but takes time the UPS batteries do not have. EBS replicates each volume across nodes. When some replicas vanish the survivors block writes until they re-mirror onto spare capacity; a zone-wide event uses up the spare. When all replicas lose power at once, consistency of recent writes cannot be proven, so AWS rebuilt those volumes as recovery snapshots via S3 rather than risk silent corruption. Its fix was to recover volumes in place on the servers. Separately, an EBS snapshot-cleanup bug, unrelated to the power event, had deleted blocks still referenced by some customer snapshots on 5 August; AWS rebuilt those by 16:19 PDT on 8 August.

Lessons learnt

  • A generator fleet is only as available as the controller that parallels it. A single PLC must not be able to keep healthy generators off the bus.
  • Network gear needs power as surely as servers: restored instances were useless for two hours while the network stayed dark.
  • Regional control planes must stop routing to a failed zone at once; queuing work for it spreads the failure to every zone.
  • Recovery capacity must be sized for the zone-wide case, not for routine node failures.
  • Being conservative about data consistency was right, but the slow recovery path turned a three-hour power event into three days for some customers.
  • Customers running independently in several zones saw no interruption.

Improvements

  • Power: correct PLC isolation and add a cold, isolated backup PLC (AWS committed).
  • Control plane: faster removal of failed API hosts and per-zone isolation (AWS committed / in progress).
  • Storage: recover EBS volumes in place after power returns (AWS committed).
  • Database: RDS Multi-AZ health checks and edge-case handling (AWS committed).
  • Communication: faster Support staffing and per-resource impairment visibility (AWS committed).
  • Analyst recommendation (not in the summary): test generator paralleling with the PLC failed, and give network gear its own protected supply priority.
