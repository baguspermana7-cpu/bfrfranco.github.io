Cooling by the Numbers

Critical metrics that define data center thermal management performance worldwide.

1.07
Hyperscale PUE Leader
The best-in-class hyperscale facilities achieve PUE of 1.07, approaching theoretical minimum. This is only possible with liquid cooling, warm-water economizers, and near-zero air-side losses.
1.8 L/kWh
WUE Industry Benchmark
Water Usage Effectiveness of 1.8 L/kWh is the global industry average. Hyperscale leaders with liquid cooling reach below 0.5 L/kWh. Facilities in water-stressed regions are targeting WUE <1.0.
18-27°C
ASHRAE A1 Recommended
ASHRAE TC 9.9 A1 recommended inlet temperature envelope. Widening to A3/A4 (up to 45°C allowable) enables more free-cooling hours but increases equipment failure probability.
30-40%
Energy for Cooling
Cooling infrastructure typically consumes 30–40% of total data center energy, making it the single largest non-IT energy consumer and the primary lever for PUE reduction.
7,000+
Free-Cooling Hours/yr
Northern European facilities achieve 7,000+ free-cooling hours per year — air-side economizers running without mechanical refrigeration. Equatorial facilities may see fewer than 500, making liquid cooling the default for tropical deployments.
60-100°C
Heat Reuse Temperature
Liquid-cooled data centers return water at 50–65°C (D2C) or 60–70°C (immersion) — hot enough for district heating networks. Several European hyperscale operators now supply waste heat to municipal heating grids year-round.
A1–A4
3 Climate Classes
ASHRAE TC 9.9 defines four IT equipment environment classes (A1–A4). Higher classes permit wider temperature envelopes, enabling free cooling in hotter climates at the cost of reduced equipment reliability margins. Class must be declared in design documentation.
50%+
Cooling % of Non-IT OpEx
Cooling infrastructure represents 50%+ of non-IT operational expenditure. A PUE improvement from 1.58 to 1.30 at a 10 MW IT load saves approximately $1–2 million annually at $0.08–0.12/kWh, making it the highest-ROI operational optimisation target.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about data center cooling and thermal management.

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is the ratio of total facility energy to IT equipment energy. A PUE of 1.0 means all energy goes to computing with zero overhead. The global average sits at 1.58, meaning roughly 37% of energy is consumed by cooling, lighting, and power distribution. Reducing PUE by 0.1 at a 10 MW facility saves $200,000–400,000 annually, making it the single most important efficiency metric for data center operators.
Every watt of IT power generates heat that must be removed to prevent equipment failure. With rack densities increasing from 5 kW (traditional) to 30+ kW (high density) and AI/GPU clusters reaching 100+ kW, cooling demand scales proportionally. Compressors, pumps, fans, and cooling towers all consume significant power. In tropical climates without free cooling, the overhead is even higher because outdoor temperatures rarely drop below the ASHRAE recommended 18°C supply air temperature.
Consider liquid cooling when rack densities exceed 25–30 kW, when PUE targets require sub-1.3 performance, or when deploying GPU/AI workloads. Direct liquid cooling can handle 100+ kW per rack with PUE values of 1.03–1.1. The transition decision depends on density requirements, local climate, water availability, retrofit costs, and total cost of ownership over a 10–15 year lifecycle. Many operators adopt a hybrid approach — air cooling for standard IT rows and liquid cooling for high-density AI zones.
Direct-to-chip (D2C) routes coolant through cold plates attached to CPUs and GPUs inside a standard server chassis, while peripheral components remain air-cooled. Immersion cooling submerges entire servers in dielectric fluid — single-phase (continuously wet) or two-phase (fluid boils off chips and condenses above). Immersion achieves near-zero airside energy, but requires full IT re-engineering for two-phase, higher fluid costs, and complex maintenance. D2C is the dominant choice for retrofit scenarios and existing hardware platforms.
ASHRAE TC 9.9 defines four classes. A1 (enterprise servers): recommended inlet 18–27°C, allowable 15–32°C, humidity 8–80% RH. A2 (volume/blade): recommended 18–27°C, allowable 10–35°C. A3 (storage, networking): allowable 5–40°C. A4 (edge, industrial): allowable 5–45°C. Higher classes enable more free-cooling hours at the cost of reduced equipment reliability margins. The declared class must appear in design documentation for ASHRAE compliance.
Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE) measures annual water consumed (litres) per kWh of IT energy delivered. The industry benchmark is around 1.8 L/kWh; hyperscale leaders with liquid cooling reach below 0.5 L/kWh. PUE captures energy efficiency while WUE captures water sustainability — a facility can achieve PUE 1.2 yet high WUE if it relies on evaporative cooling in a water-stressed region. Both metrics together give a complete picture of resource efficiency and are used in Green Grid reporting.
Relative humidity (RH) expresses moisture as a percentage of air's maximum capacity at a given temperature — it changes as temperature changes, even if moisture content does not. Dew point is the absolute temperature at which condensation forms, independent of air temperature. ASHRAE recommends controlling to a dew point of 5.5–15°C rather than %RH because RH swings dangerously during cooling transients. Dew-point sensors at rack inlets provide the more reliable control signal for preventing condensation events during economizer transitions.
Free cooling removes heat using outdoor air or cooling-tower water directly, bypassing mechanical refrigeration compressors. Air-side economisers introduce outdoor air when cooler and drier than IT exhaust; water-side economisers circulate chilled water through a heat exchanger when outdoor wet-bulb temperature is below supply water set point. Northern European facilities achieve 7,000+ free-cooling hours per year. Equatorial facilities may see fewer than 500. Widening the ASHRAE class to A3/A4 can add 1,000–2,000 free-cooling hours in temperate climates.
When water access is restricted, closed-loop mechanical cooling with dry coolers minimises consumption. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling with a closed-loop CDU and air-blast dry cooler is highly effective — it circulates facility water without evaporative loss. Two-phase immersion uses dielectric fluid in a closed loop with near-zero water consumption. Avoid open-circuit cooling towers and direct evaporative systems. Many hyperscalers now set a WUE ceiling of 1.0 L/kWh for new sites in water-stressed regions, as defined by WRI Aqueduct baseline water stress data.
Yes. Data center waste heat is viable for district heating when return temperatures exceed 60°C — achievable with D2C cooling (supply 30–45°C, return 50–65°C) or immersion (return 60–70°C). Stockholm Data Parks, Equinix Paris, and several Nordic operators supply waste heat to municipal heating networks, recovering 60–100% of server heat output. Air-cooled facilities at 40°C exhaust are marginal; liquid-cooled facilities with warm-water returns are highly compatible with existing district heat infrastructure.
Common configurations: N (no redundancy), N+1 (one spare chiller, standard for Tier III), 2N (two fully independent chiller plants, Tier IV), N+2 (two spares for very high availability). Tier III requires concurrent maintainability — the cooling system must carry full IT load while one component is under maintenance. Chiller redundancy must be matched by redundant cooling towers, condenser water pumps, chilled water pumps, and piping headers. For AI/HPC deployments where cooling failure causes immediate GPU throttle, N+1 minimum is the baseline; 2N is increasingly standard.
EU Regulation 2024/573 phases down high-GWP HFC refrigerants used in CRAC and chiller compressors. R-410A (GWP 2,088) and R-134a (GWP 1,430) face significant quota reductions through 2030. Low-GWP alternatives include R-32 (GWP 675), R-454B (GWP 466), and R-1234ze (GWP <1). New EU cooling plant should specify HFC-free or low-GWP refrigerants. Annual leak checks are mandatory for systems containing 5+ tonnes CO₂-equivalent. Existing systems may continue with recycled refrigerant but refill availability will tighten significantly through the decade.
Hot and cold aisle containment physically separates cooled supply air from hot exhaust air, preventing recirculation. Cold aisle containment (CAC) encloses the front-of-rack intake path; hot aisle containment (HAC) encloses the rear exhaust and ducts it to CRAC returns. Both require blanking panels in every empty rack U-space, brush-grommet cable seals at all cutouts, and raised-floor tile placement matched to actual thermal load. With containment, supply temperature can typically be raised 2–4°C, saving significant compressor energy and increasing free-cooling hours.
BMS integrates chiller staging, cooling tower fans, CRAH controls, and PDU power measurements into a unified control layer. Advanced BMS implementations pre-cool during off-peak tariff windows, optimise chilled water delta-T, and stage economiser changeover based on forecast ambient conditions. Integration with DCIM allows rack-level power data to drive proactive CRAH adjustments before thermal events occur. Alarm thresholds should be reviewed quarterly — stale setpoints are a leading cause of thermal incidents during unusual workload patterns such as AI training burst loads.
Cooling infrastructure represents 30–40% of total facility energy cost and 50%+ of non-IT operational expenditure. At a PUE of 1.58, cooling and other overhead consume 37% of all facility energy. A move from PUE 1.58 to 1.30 at a 10 MW IT load saves approximately 2.8 MW of cooling power — worth $1–2 million annually at $0.08–0.12/kWh. Cooling OpEx is therefore the primary target for energy efficiency investment in any data center optimisation programme.

      Heat-Removal Path — 5 Tiers

      From silicon junction to facility boundary: how heat moves through a data center and where each cooling technology acts.

      Tier 1 Chip Silicon junction · TDP · Throttle threshold ▼
      Heat originates at transistor junctions inside the CPU or GPU die. Junction temperature (Tj) must stay below the thermal design maximum — typically 80–105°C for enterprise CPUs, 83–90°C for datacenter GPUs. Thermal Design Power (TDP) is the maximum sustained heat output the cooling solution must handle. If Tj exceeds the threshold, the processor throttles frequency and voltage to reduce heat, degrading performance. Typical Tier 1 ΔT: junction to case ~10–25°C.
      • Technologies: Integrated heat spreader (IHS), thermal interface material (TIM), die backside metallisation
      • ΔT typical: 10–25°C (junction to case lid)
      Tier 2 Server Heat sink · Server fans · Cold-plate ▼
      Inside the server chassis, heat is transferred from the chip package to either an air-cooled heat sink (with server fans forcing air across fins) or a liquid cold plate (circulating coolant through channels bonded to the chip package). Air-cooled servers exhaust 40–60°C air. Cold-plate liquid servers return 50–65°C facility water. GPU modules with TDP of 300–1,000 W each require high-airflow or liquid-cooled cold plates to prevent throttling.
      • Air path: Heat sink fins + server fans (PWM 6,000–20,000 RPM) → 40–60°C exhaust
      • Liquid path: Cold plate → coolant manifold → CDU return at 50–65°C
      • ΔT typical: 15–35°C (case to exhaust air or coolant return)
      Tier 3 Rack CDU on rack · In-row CRAH · Rear-door HX ▼
      At the rack level, heat is managed by the combination of containment, in-row cooling units, rear-door heat exchangers, or rack-mounted CDUs. In-row CRAH units sit between rows and capture hot exhaust before it mixes with cold supply. Rear-door HX attach to the back of racks and cool exhaust to near-neutral temperature before it enters the hot aisle. Rack CDUs receive liquid from floor manifolds and distribute to cold plates inside the rack. Containment walls prevent hot/cold mixing.
      • In-row CRAH: Reduces hot aisle temperature from 55–70°C to 28–35°C
      • Rear-door HX: Cools exhaust from 55°C to 25–30°C using chilled water at 12–18°C supply
      • CDU supply: 30–45°C; return: 50–65°C; ΔT across rack ~15–20°C
      Tier 4 Room CRAC/CRAH · Raised floor · Containment ▼
      Room-level cooling units (CRAC — Computer Room Air Conditioner with internal DX refrigeration; CRAH — Computer Room Air Handler that uses chilled water) manage the overall thermal balance of the data hall. The raised floor acts as a pressurised plenum delivering cold air through perforated tiles at floor level to the front of racks. Containment systems (cold aisle or hot aisle) prevent mixing. Room temperature should be maintained uniformly at the ASHRAE recommended envelope without hot spots above 27°C.
      • CRAC: Self-contained DX refrigeration; supply 18–20°C; less efficient at high ambient
      • CRAH: Uses chilled water from central plant; supply 12–18°C; more efficient; requires plant redundancy
      • ΔT typical (room): 8–12°C supply to return at CRAC/CRAH coil
      Tier 5 Facility Chiller · Cooling tower · Dry cooler · Economiser ▼
      The facility boundary is where heat is ultimately rejected to the environment. Chillers use vapour-compression refrigeration to produce chilled water (6–12°C supply) for the CRAH/RDHX loops. Cooling towers reject condenser heat to atmosphere via evaporation. Dry coolers (closed-circuit fluid coolers) reject heat without evaporative water loss. Air-side economisers bring outdoor air directly into the data hall during cool weather. Warm-water economisers (water-side economisers) use a plate heat exchanger to produce chilled water from cooling tower water when outdoor wet-bulb temperatures are low enough.
      • Chiller CoP: 3.0–6.0 depending on load and ambient; modern magnetic bearing chillers reach CoP 8+ at part load
      • Cooling tower WUE impact: 1.5–3.0 L/kWh of heat rejected (evaporation + drift + bleed)
      • Air-side economiser: Zero refrigeration energy when active; typical annual hours: 500 (tropics) to 7,000+ (northern Europe)

      Cooling Case Studies

      Three real-world deployments demonstrating advanced cooling technologies and their operational outcomes.

      Singapore — 2022

      Meta Singapore — Equatorial Indirect Adiabatic Cooling

      Meta's Singapore data center became the first hyperscale facility to deploy indirect adiabatic cooling in an equatorial climate, where ambient temperatures rarely drop below 28°C and humidity exceeds 80% year-round. The design uses an indirect evaporative cooling stage that pre-cools outdoor air without introducing moisture into the IT space.

      Key result: PUE 1.19 achieved despite 30°C+ ambient — significantly below Singapore average of ~1.55 for conventional colo. ASHRAE A2 envelope maintained continuously. Zero mechanical refrigeration chillers in primary cooling path.

      Key Takeaways

      • Indirect adiabatic cooling is viable in tropical climates when supply air humidity is carefully separated from the evaporative stage
      • Widening the IT envelope to ASHRAE A2 (allowable 10–35°C) was essential to achieve the free-cooling hours required for the PUE target
      • Water consumption (WUE) remains a trade-off — adiabatic cooling consumes more water than mechanical refrigeration; site water management plan was mandatory
      Scottish Coast, UK — 2018–2020

      Microsoft Project Natick — Underwater Nitrogen-Atmosphere Cooling

      Microsoft submerged a sealed 12-rack data center module on the seabed off the Orkney Islands for two years, cooling it passively with seawater piped through a heat exchanger. The nitrogen atmosphere (rather than air) inside the vessel eliminated humidity and oxygen-related corrosion and virtually eliminated hardware failures.

      Key result: Hardware failure rate was 1/8th of equivalent land-based deployment over 2 years. Seawater at 12°C provided near-free cooling. Nitrogen atmosphere reduced corrosion and eliminated moisture-related failures — demonstrating that the atmosphere, not just temperature, drives equipment reliability.

      Key Takeaways

      • Controlled atmosphere (nitrogen, zero humidity) is as important as temperature for hardware reliability — a lesson applicable to sealed immersion tank design
      • Passive seawater cooling achieves near-zero PUE overhead and zero water consumption (closed-loop seawater heat exchange, no evaporation)
      • Deployment logistics and maintenance access remain the primary barriers to commercial scaling of submerged or remote hermetic designs
      Strasbourg, France — 2021–2023

      OVHcloud Strasbourg — Adiabatic Free Cooling Post-SBG2 Rebuild

      Following the catastrophic March 2021 SBG2 fire that destroyed one data center building and damaged three others, OVHcloud rebuilt the Strasbourg campus with structural compartmentation specifically designed to prevent fire spread and with adiabatic free cooling as the primary cooling strategy for the replacement SBG5 building.

      Key result: SBG5 achieved PUE of 1.09 using air-side adiabatic economisers and Strasbourg's temperate climate (5,500+ free-cooling hours/year). The rebuild incorporated both fire compartmentation and thermal architecture lessons simultaneously — demonstrating that cooling redesign and fire safety redesign are coupled decisions in post-incident rebuilds.

      Key Takeaways

      • Temperate European climates enable adiabatic free cooling with minimal water consumption — Strasbourg's climate allows economiser operation for most of the year
      • Compartmentation rebuilt simultaneously with cooling architecture — fire separation between modules reduces both fire risk and CRAC/CRAH zone complexity
      • Post-incident rebuilds present the best opportunity to adopt next-generation cooling because the structural investment is being made regardless of efficiency gain

      Cooling Glossary

      14 key terms in data center thermal management — from efficiency metrics to physical phenomena.

      PUE
      Power Usage Effectiveness — ratio of total facility power to IT equipment power. PUE = 1.0 is theoretical perfection; global average is 1.58.
      WUE
      Water Usage Effectiveness — litres of water consumed per kWh of IT energy. Industry benchmark ~1.8 L/kWh; liquid-cooled hyperscale leaders achieve <0.5 L/kWh.
      CRAH
      Computer Room Air Handler — a precision cooling unit that uses chilled water from a central plant, rather than a built-in compressor. More efficient than CRAC at scale; requires chiller plant redundancy.
      CRAC
      Computer Room Air Conditioner — a self-contained precision cooling unit with an integrated DX refrigeration compressor. Can operate independently but less efficient than centralised chilled water at large scale.
      CDU
      Coolant Distribution Unit — the interface between the facility chilled-water loop and the rack-level liquid cooling loop. Contains pumps, heat exchangers, and flow controls for distributing coolant to cold plates or manifolds.
      D2C (Direct-to-Chip)
      Liquid cooling method where coolant circulates through cold plates bonded directly to CPU/GPU packages inside a standard server chassis. Peripheral components remain air-cooled. Enables 30–100 kW/rack densities.
      TDP
      Thermal Design Power — the maximum sustained heat a processor generates under full workload, which the cooling solution must continuously remove without throttling. Measured in watts; ranges from 250 W (server CPU) to 1,000 W (AI accelerator).
      ΔT (Delta-T)
      Temperature difference between two measurement points — e.g., the temperature rise across a rack from front inlet to rear exhaust, or the chilled water supply-to-return temperature differential. Critical for sizing cooling equipment and detecting airflow problems.
      Free Cooling
      Using outdoor air or cool ambient water to remove data center heat without operating mechanical refrigeration compressors — eliminating compressor energy during qualifying weather conditions. Also called economiser mode.
      Economiser
      System or operating mode that bypasses mechanical refrigeration by using outdoor conditions directly. Air-side economisers introduce outdoor air; water-side economisers use a heat exchanger between cooling tower water and chilled water loop when ambient wet-bulb temperature is sufficiently low.
      Dew Point
      The temperature at which air becomes saturated and water vapour begins to condense. ASHRAE recommends maintaining a dew point of 5.5–15°C in IT spaces. Below 5.5°C risks static electricity damage; above 15°C risks condensation on cooled surfaces.
      Adiabatic Cooling
      Cooling by evaporating water into incoming air, absorbing heat as latent energy. Direct adiabatic introduces moisture directly; indirect adiabatic pre-cools air on the wet side of a plate heat exchanger without adding moisture to the supply air. Used in data centers to extend free-cooling hours in hot climates.
      Immersion (Single-Phase)
      Servers fully submerged in a bath of non-evaporating dielectric fluid (engineered mineral oil or synthetic fluid). Fluid absorbs heat by convection and is pumped to an external dry cooler or heat exchanger. Enables 100+ kW/rack. Fluid remains liquid throughout; servers stay continuously wet.
      Immersion (Two-Phase)
      Servers submerged in a low-boiling-point dielectric fluid (fluorinated hydrocarbon, e.g., 3M Novec). Fluid boils directly off chips, capturing latent heat; vapour condenses on a water-cooled condenser above the tank and drips back. Achieves highest thermal density with near-zero pump energy but requires fluorinated fluid containment and OEM re-engineering.
