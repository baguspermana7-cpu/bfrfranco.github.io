Google Cloud europe-west4-a: Grid Transient, DRUPS Failure and Chiller Shutdown

Google Cloud (data center provider not named) 2026-07-15 14h 55m to substantial restoration PowerCooling
SummaryTimelineResponseFMEACausesActionsSources

Summary

On 15 July 2026 a 3 ms grid transient tripped both utility feeds at the facility hosting Google Cloud zone europe-west4-a. The side A rotary UPS failed, one row lost power on transfer, and the chiller controller dropped out so the pumps stopped while the backup cooling was out for construction. The hall reached 44°C and equipment was shut down; customer disruption lasted 14 hours 55 minutes.

Root cause and contributing factors

TRIGGER: a 3 ms voltage transient on the utility grid upstream of the data center hosting europe-west4-a tripped the utility breakers on both feeds A and B at 16:24 PDT on 15 July 2026, and the site transferred to its diesel rotary UPS (DRUPS). POWER CHAIN: side B went to DRUPS cleanly. The side A DRUPS failed to take over the facility load due to electrical component failures, so the three rows on feed A had to transfer automatically to feed B. Two rows did.
Full root-cause narrative
Row 3 hit an overload protection breaker trip and lost both feeds; Google attributes this to a load deployment discrepancy, still under investigation at the time of the report. COOLING CHAIN: the same transient knocked the chiller controller offline. The chillers kept power but the chilled-water distribution pumps stopped and could not restart, so with no water flow chiller system A shut down. The redundant cooling source was unavailable because of known ongoing construction work at the facility. The hall reached 44°C two hours later and the equipment had to be shut down. LATENT ROOT: three independent weaknesses lined up on one transient: a DRUPS that could not carry its load, a row whose load did not fit the surviving feed, and a cooling plant whose controls had no ride-through and whose redundancy had been removed for construction. The facility team's own fix, a portable UPS for the chiller controllers, indicates the controllers were not on protected power (analyst inference from the action taken).

Contributing factors by category

  • The side A DRUPS failed to take the load because of electrical component failures (Google report).
  • Row 3 tripped an overload protection breaker on transfer to feed B: a load deployment discrepancy, still under investigation (Google report).
  • The chiller controller dropped offline on the transient and the distribution pumps did not restart on their own (Google report).
  • The redundant cooling source was out of service because of known ongoing construction work (Google report).
  • Chiller controllers apparently lacked protected power: the fix was an interim portable UPS for them (analyst inference from the action).
  • Console servers needed an onsite technician to recover, which delayed network restoration after cooling returned (Google report, GCVE timeline).
  • Thermal shutdown was a manual procedure; Google is now automating shutdown for fast-moving thermal events (Google report).

Analysis

One transient, three failures

A 3 ms dip should be a non-event for a site with two feeds, a rotary UPS on each side and redundant cooling. Here it found a failed DRUPS on side A, an overloaded row that could not move to side B, and a chiller controller with no ride-through while the second cooling source was out for construction. Each weakness alone would have been survivable.

Cooling failed through its controls

The chillers kept power. What stopped was the controller and therefore the pumps. The repair was to put the pumps in hand mode. Controls and pumps belong in the same protected-power and restart design as the chillers, or cooling capacity on paper is not capacity in an event.

Construction as a hidden degradation

The redundant cooling source was out for known construction work. Planned degradation is common, but the record shows no compensating measure for the window, and the event happened inside it.

Speed of a thermal event

From transient to 44°C took about two hours; NetApp clusters shut themselves down after about an hour. Google ran manual shutdown procedures and is now automating them, and is changing incident classification so enough people are available during major data center events.

What the record does not say

The report does not name the data center provider, the DRUPS make or the failed components, the chiller or pump models, or the cost. It also does not say whether the committed actions due in August to October 2026 have been completed.

Technical deep-dive
A rotary UPS bridges the gap to diesel with a flywheel; if its electrical components fail, the side it protects drops to the automatic transfer scheme. That scheme only works if every row's load fits the surviving feed: Row 3 tripped its overload protection, the textbook symptom of a row loaded beyond what one feed can carry alone (Google calls it a load deployment discrepancy). The cooling failure is a controls failure, not a chiller failure. A brief transient reset or dropped the chiller controller; the pumps needed a restart command that never came. The quick fix was mechanical: pumps put in hand mode. With the redundant source out for construction there was no second loop, so the hall rose to 44°C in about two hours and Google shut down reachable equipment. Recovery then needed an onsite technician for console servers, which added time after cooling was back at 21:05.

Lessons learnt

  • A cooling plant's controls are part of the critical load. If a 3 ms transient can drop the chiller controller, the cooling plant has no ride-through no matter how much the chillers can do.
  • Pumps that need a restart command after a power event are a single point of failure; restart-on-power-return or hand-mode fallback must be designed in and drilled.
  • Taking cooling redundancy out for construction turns the next routine event into a thermal emergency. Plan compensating measures for the construction window.
  • Automatic transfer schemes only work if each row's load fits the surviving feed. Track deployed load per row against single-feed capacity.
  • Thermal events move fast: 44°C two hours after the trigger. Shutdown triggers and onsite staffing must be faster than the temperature rise.
  • Recovery depends on out-of-band access: needing a technician on site for console servers lengthened the outage after cooling returned.

Improvements

  • Safety and cooling: keep chiller controllers on protected power (interim portable UPS done) and review pump control redundancy (Google committed).
  • Designinvestigate the DRUPS transfer failure and restore full A-side redundancy (swing DRUPS in place; investigation committed).
  • Designresolve the Row 3 overload so every row fits one surviving feed (Google committed).
  • Monitoring: earlier alerts for power events and cooling excursions (Google committed).
  • Processautomated shutdown for fast thermal runaway, updated incident classification for staffing, joint runbooks and a monthly emergency drill with the data center team (Google committed).
  • Analyst recommendation (not in the report): treat removal of cooling redundancy for construction as a change that needs a compensating plan, such as temporary chillers or reduced load.
