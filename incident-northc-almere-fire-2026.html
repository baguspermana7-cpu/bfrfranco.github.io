NorthC Almere Data Center Fire: A Compartment Blaze That Took Out the Facility Power Plant and Rippled Across Dutch Public Services

NorthC Datacenters 2026-05-07 144h 0m core impact FirePower

On 7 May 2026 (around 08:45 local time) a fire broke out in a rear technical compartment of NorthC's Almere data center (Rondebeltweg) that houses power infrastructure. No servers or data carriers caught fire, but the blaze burned part of the facility's power supply and the site lost power entirely — the compute survived, yet everything went dark, and NorthC ultimately had to rebuild the power plant with new transformers and UPS. (Published reporting says only that 'part of the power supply burnt down'; that BOTH the normal and standby feeds were lost is an engineering inference from the total, multi-day power loss and the full rebuild, not an official finding.) Because multiple Dutch organizations host critical systems there, the outage cascaded outward: Utrecht University, Statistics Netherlands (CBS), public-transport operator Transdev, a regional water board, research-ICT provider Surf, IBM Cloud's Amsterdam 03, and numerous GP practices all lost service. The fire was scaled down to GRIP 1 at 20:50 that evening and to GRIP 0 with the building partially released to NorthC teams at 00:35 on 8 May, kicking off a phased restoration estimated at up to 72 hours. Temporary redundant power and cooling were activated on 13 May, roughly a week after the fire. Grid power came back building-by-building over the following weeks, with Building 1's full utility-plus-standby topology targeted for 17 July 2026. The root cause remains officially undetermined; a draft cause report was only expected at end-September 2026 because the fire-damaged structure had to be secured first.

Root cause

No official root cause has been published for the NorthC Almere (Rondebeltweg) data center fire of 2026-05-07. NorthC states that nothing can be said with certainty about the cause, and the cause investigation is being carried out by several independent experts whose draft report was deferred to end of September 2026. The trigger/ignition source is therefore UNDETERMINED, and any statement of ignition cause would be speculation. What follows is a reconstruction of the established failure mechanism from the ignition location onward, not of the ignition cause itself. Location: The fire broke out at the rear of the building, "where the technical facilities are located" — the power-infrastructure zone, not the raised-floor data halls. The specific ignition source within that technical area has not been established by any authoritative body. Mechanism (why electrical redundancy did not survive): The decisive engineering fact is that the fire physically consumed the power plant. Structure Research reports the fire "took down both primary and backup power systems." Because the primary and backup power equipment for Almere 1 were co-located within the affected rear technical zone, the event manifested as a common-cause (common-mode) failure: the same physical fire that destroyed the primary path also destroyed the redundant/backup path, so N+1 or 2N electrical redundancy provided no protection. Redundancy defends against the failure of one path, not against destruction of the shared physical room housing all paths. Why propagation and life-safety safeguards DID work: Fire compartmentation performed as designed — smoke in the halls remained limited and the fire compartmentation prevented the smoke from spreading further. The blaze was confined to the technical facilities; the Almere 2 technical room sustained no fire damage, while only Almere 1 required new transformers and UPS systems. Detection and evacuation also worked: all occupants were evacuated in time with no injuries. Escalation and recovery: The incident was escalated under the Dutch GRIP emergency-coordination scheme with an NL-Alert issued, then de-escalated to GRIP 1 at 20:50 on May 7 and to GRIP 0 at 00:35 on May 8. Because the on-site power plant was destroyed rather than merely tripped, recovery could not be achieved by failover; it required physically bridging the site with a large temporary plant — a large number of generators, UPS systems and distribution units, and laying more than a kilometer of cable — activated 2026-05-13. Grid power was restored in phases (Almere 2 reconnected May 29; Almere 1 partially by end of June and fully through July). In summary: life-safety and compartmentation safeguards succeeded; the electrical-redundancy safeguard failed by common-cause destruction of the co-located primary and backup power infrastructure; and the ignition cause remains officially undetermined.

Contributing factors

  • Co-location of primary and backup power infrastructure in a single rear technical zone, creating a common-cause failure surface: the same fire that destroyed the primary supply also destroyed the backup, so electrical redundancy provided no protection (Structure Research: fire 'took down both primary and backup power systems').
  • The fire originated in the technical facilities / power-infrastructure area itself ('the rear of the building, where the technical facilities are located'), so the failure struck the very subsystem meant to guarantee power continuity rather than an ancillary space.
  • Severity and physical destruction of the plant meant recovery depended on a slow, manual bridging effort rather than automatic failover — installing a large number of generators, UPS systems and distribution units and laying more than a kilometer of cable (temporary plant energized 2026-05-13) — extending the outage across multiple days.
  • Site instability and severe structural damage to Almere 1 delayed both restoration and the root-cause investigation (draft expert report deferred to end of September 2026), leaving the ignition source undetermined for an extended period and preventing timely corrective learning.
  • Single-site power dependency: destruction of one facility's power plant resulted in service outages for directly affected downstream customers during the multi-day recovery, illustrating concentration risk (specific affected organizations were not named by NorthC).

Lessons learnt

  • A single compartment fire produced a total, multi-day facility power loss requiring a full power-plant rebuild — apparent evidence that co-locating primary and backup power lets one event defeat the redundancy, though the both-feeds reading is inferred from the recovery record rather than an official finding.
  • A facility-level power loss cascades immediately to any tenant that lacks independent local generation or an alternate site; the fire never touched the servers, yet the servers still went dark.
  • Concentrating critical public services — national statistics, healthcare records, public transport, water management, higher education — in one data center turns a single-site incident into a nationwide disruption.
  • When a fire causes structural damage, root-cause analysis can be delayed by months because investigators cannot safely enter until the building is secured, so incident communications must manage a long 'cause unknown' period.
  • Transparency gaps compound impact: the absence of a published affected-customer count or per-organization outage figures leaves affected users and the public without a clear picture of scope.

Improvements & remediation

  • Physically separate and fire-compartmentalize the primary and backup power paths so no single fire zone can destroy both, and review detection/suppression coverage in technical/power compartments.
  • Encourage or require geographic redundancy and disaster-recovery failover for tenants running critical public-sector workloads, rather than single-site dependency.
  • Establish clearer, faster public impact reporting (tenant/customer scope, outage windows) during and after major incidents.
  • Harden and stage the power-restoration sequence (temporary power/cooling first, then per-building grid, then full utility-plus-standby topology) with pre-provisioned transformers and UPS to shorten recovery.
  • Conduct a post-incident structural/safety review so that future fire-damaged sites can be entered and investigated sooner, shortening the time to root cause.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism: a power-plant fire, not a server fire

The defining feature of this incident is that the IT load never burned — the fire was in a rear technical compartment that housed power infrastructure, and it burned part of the facility's power supply, producing a total loss of power. The servers were physically intact but electrically dead. This inverts the usual mental model of a data center fire (racks ablaze) and points instead to the power plant as the single most concentrated point of failure: lose it, and a fully healthy compute estate becomes worthless. Published reporting confirms only that 'part of the power supply burnt down'; that both the normal and standby feeds were lost is inferred from the total, multi-day outage and the need to rebuild with new transformers and UPS. Because the root cause is officially undetermined, the mechanism of ignition remains unknown pending the end-September 2026 draft report.

Why it cascaded across Dutch public life

The outage propagated because of dependency concentration, not because the fire spread. Numerous organizations run production systems at the Almere site with no evident local generation or cross-site failover, so a facility power loss became their outage instantly. The victim list is unusually broad for a single building — Utrecht University (down to access passes), Statistics Netherlands, Transdev's buses and trams, a regional water board, Surf's research-ICT members, IBM Cloud's Amsterdam 03, and GP practices locked out of patient records and appointment data. The societal reach came from who was hosted there, turning an infrastructure event into a public-services event.

Recovery: staged over days to weeks

Recovery followed a deliberate ladder. The emergency was controlled the same evening — safety-region all-clear at 20:13 and GRIP 1 at 20:50 on 7 May — and the building was scaled to GRIP 0 and partially released to NorthC teams at 00:35 on 8 May with a phased restoration estimated at up to 72 hours. Temporary redundant power and cooling were activated on 13 May, roughly a week later, in what reporting framed as a six-day facility outage. Durable grid power then came back building-by-building: Building 2 in the week of 25 May, Building 1 partially by end June, with full utility-plus-standby topology (new transformers and UPS, generators as backup) targeted for 17 July 2026 — a date announced in the operator log but not independently confirmed as executed.

Organizational and systemic factors

Two systemic issues stand out. First, redundancy appears to have been defeated by proximity: the total, multi-day power loss and full rebuild imply the primary and backup power were vulnerable to the same event, so the architecture that should have survived a single failure did not (an inference, since press reported only that 'part of the power supply burnt down'). Second, critical national functions were concentrated at one facility with insufficient tenant-side resilience, so the blast radius was national. The record also shows a transparency and investigation lag — no affected-customer count, per-organization outage duration, or damages figure was ever published, and the cause report was delayed to end-September 2026 because the damaged structure had to be secured before investigators could safely enter.

Cross-incident pattern: the concentrated-power single point of failure

This event rhymes with a recurring class of data center outages in which the compute is fine but a shared upstream utility — power distribution, cooling, or a single electrical room — fails and takes everything with it. The lesson repeats across the industry: redundancy only counts if the redundant paths are independent in space and failure mode, and tenant resilience cannot be outsourced entirely to a single facility. The Almere fire is a particularly clean example because the servers demonstrably survived, isolating the failure to the power plant and the dependency graph hanging off it.

Technical deep-dive

The established failure mechanism is a common-cause (common-mode) loss of the electrical power chain, not a failure of automatic transfer or of data-hall protection. Data center power resilience is normally built on redundant paths (utility feed plus generator/UPS backup, arranged N+1 or 2N) so that the failure of any one component or path triggers failover to a healthy path. That model assumes the failure is localized to a component, not a destruction of the shared physical environment that houses all paths. Here the fire occurred inside the rear technical facilities — the room(s) containing the power supply, transformers and UPS systems. Structure Research's account that the event 'took down both primary and backup power systems' is the diagnostic signature of a common-cause event: when primary and backup equipment share a fire compartment, a single fire defeats every redundant path simultaneously, so no amount of N+1/2N redundancy prevents total power loss. By contrast, the compartmentation and life-safety layers behaved exactly as engineered. Fire compartmentation (fire-rated separation between the technical zone and the white space) kept smoke in the halls limited and prevented further spread; the Almere 2 technical room took no fire damage, demonstrating that compartment boundaries held and localized the damage to Almere 1's power plant. Detection and evacuation completed with no injuries. The recovery path is itself evidence of the mechanism: because the plant was destroyed rather than merely tripped, restoration required physically rebuilding a temporary power plant (a large number of generators, UPS systems and distribution units and more than a kilometer of cable) energized on 2026-05-13, followed by phased grid reconnection (Almere 2 on May 29) and, for Almere 1, installation of new transformers and UPS systems through late June and July. The one gap in this deep-dive is the ignition source: no official body has determined what started the fire, NorthC states nothing can be said with certainty about the cause, and an independent-expert investigation is ongoing with a draft report deferred to end of September 2026, so the mechanism is reconstructed only from the ignition location onward. A claim circulated in secondary reporting that the fire brigade requested a power shutdown could not be verified against an accessible source and is not asserted here.
