Power by the Numbers

Critical metrics that define data center electrical infrastructure performance.

96-97%
UPS Efficiency
Modern online double-conversion UPS systems achieve 96-97% efficiency. Eco-mode can push this to 99%, but at the cost of slightly longer transfer times during power events.
10s
Generator Startup
Diesel generators typically reach full load acceptance within 10 seconds of a power failure. During this window, UPS batteries bridge the gap to maintain continuous power to IT loads.
110%
2N CAPEX Premium
Fully redundant 2N power architecture adds up to 110% to capital costs versus N+1. This premium buys concurrently maintainable independent power paths with no single points of failure.
99.995%
Tier IV Uptime
Tier IV facilities target 99.995% uptime (26 min/yr). Tier III targets 99.982% (1.6 hr/yr). Achieving Tier IV requires fully fault-tolerant 2N design throughout the entire power chain.
3×
Lithium vs VRLA Density
Lithium-ion UPS batteries achieve roughly 3× the energy density of VRLA at the same footprint, and last 10-15 years versus 4-6 years for VRLA — delivering lower total 10-year TCO despite higher upfront cost.
72 hr
Fuel Autonomy Standard
NFPA 110 Level 1 requires a minimum 96-hour fuel supply; Uptime Institute and most hyperscale operators specify 72-hour autonomy at full load. Fuel polishing is mandatory to achieve this in practice.
40 cal/cm²
Arc Flash Hazard
Incident energy at main data center switchgear can reach 40 cal/cm² or higher without zone-selective interlocking or current-limiting fuses. NFPA 70E §130.5 mandates arc flash risk assessment before any energized work.
<5 Ω
Ground Fault Resistance
IEEE 142 and NEC §250 require data center grounding systems to maintain ground fault resistance below 5 Ω (typically <1 Ω for sensitive IT environments). Ground integrity tests should be performed annually and after any major power infrastructure change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about data center power and electrical systems.

N+1 redundancy means deploying one additional component beyond the minimum needed (N) to carry the full IT load. For example, if 4 UPS modules are required for full capacity, an N+1 design installs 5 modules. If any single module fails or is taken offline for maintenance, the remaining 4 still handle the entire load without interruption. This approach adds roughly 20-25% to capital costs versus a non-redundant design, offering a practical balance between reliability and budget.
An online double-conversion UPS continuously converts incoming AC power to DC through a rectifier, stores energy in batteries (lithium-ion or VRLA), then converts back to clean AC power through an inverter. This topology provides zero transfer time during outages and isolates IT equipment from power anomalies including voltage sags, surges, harmonics, and frequency variations. The batteries typically provide 5-15 minutes of runtime, bridging the gap until backup generators reach full load acceptance.
kW (kilowatts) measures real power, which is the actual energy consumed to perform work. kVA (kilovolt-amperes) measures apparent power, the total power drawn from the electrical circuit including reactive components. The relationship is kW = kVA multiplied by the power factor (PF). Modern IT servers typically have a PF of 0.95-0.99, so the values are nearly identical. However, UPS systems and transformers are often rated in kVA, making it critical to understand this distinction when sizing equipment to avoid overloading.
Tier III (Concurrently Maintainable) requires N+1 redundancy so any single component can be serviced without shutting down the facility — planned maintenance never causes downtime but an unplanned failure of the redundant path can still interrupt operations. Tier IV (Fault Tolerant) requires 2N redundancy with fully independent, simultaneous active power paths — any single fault or planned maintenance does not interrupt operations. Tier III targets 99.982% uptime (1.6 hr/yr) and Tier IV targets 99.995% (26 min/yr). The 2N CAPEX premium for Tier IV is typically 60-110% over Tier III.
Concurrent maintenance is the ability to take any single component in the power or cooling chain offline for planned maintenance without interrupting the IT load. It requires at least N+1 redundancy per Uptime Institute Tier III. A facility that claims concurrent maintainability must demonstrate that every component — every UPS module, PDU, ATS, cooling unit, and generator — can be individually removed from service without a cascade to the IT load. Real-world Tier III failures often trace to maintenance windows where N+1 capacity was consumed, leaving the facility running in N (no-redundancy) mode.
2N doubles capital and operating costs while adding complexity that increases probability of maintenance-induced failures, firmware version mismatches between parallel UPS strings, and ATS synchronization errors. For smaller IT loads, stranded capacity can exceed 40%, worsening PUE significantly. N+1 with disciplined maintenance procedures, aggressive alarming, and fast spare parts availability often achieves better real-world availability than a poorly maintained 2N system. 2N is justified when the IT load critically requires zero-transfer-time fault tolerance and the operator has the staffing, procedures, and test regime to maintain two fully independent systems.
Lithium-ion UPS batteries offer 3x higher energy density than VRLA, longer calendar life (10-15 years vs 4-6 years for VRLA), faster recharge (30 min vs 4-8 hours), and built-in Battery Management System monitoring. VRLA remains lower in upfront cost and is better understood by legacy maintenance teams. Lithium-ion is now the default for hyperscale and large colocation deployments due to lower 10-year TCO and smaller footprint. Thermal runaway risk with lithium-ion requires BMS integration with the fire suppression and monitoring system.
Rotary (diesel rotary / DRUPS) is preferred when: (1) zero battery footprint is required with generator-integrated flywheel ride-through; (2) extreme harmonic distortion from non-linear loads (high THD) would damage static UPS rectifiers; or (3) very high short-circuit fault current (kAIC) is required. Most modern hyperscale and colocation deployments use static double-conversion UPS with lithium-ion batteries due to lower cost, modular scalability, and simpler maintenance. DRUPS is predominantly specified for industrial, defense, and some financial trading facilities.
Diesel stored in bulk tanks for more than 6-12 months accumulates microbial growth, water condensation, and oxidation products that form sludge and clog fuel injectors. Fuel polishing — continuous or periodic filtration through fine-mesh separators with biocide treatment — removes these contaminants before they reach the injection system. Without fuel polishing, a generator may fail to sustain load during an outage despite passing routine no-load tests. Real-world incidents (Google Cloud 2019) have revealed that 72-hour autonomy assumptions were actually 24-48 hours once contaminated fuel degraded generator performance under full load.
Open-transition ATS breaks the normal source connection before connecting to the alternate source, creating a brief interruption (100-200 ms) during transfer. Closed-transition ATS momentarily parallels both sources (less than 100 ms) for a make-before-break transfer with zero interruption. Closed-transition is required for sensitive loads that cannot tolerate any gap and for generators that must be synchronized before connecting. Open-transition is simpler, lower cost, and acceptable for loads protected by double-conversion UPS that have sufficient battery bridge time.
DC bus architecture distributes power at 380 VDC (or 48 VDC at rack level) rather than the traditional AC chain. Eliminating multiple AC-DC and DC-AC conversion stages reduces losses from 4-8% to 1-2% and removes the UPS inverter stage. Hyperscale operators including Google, Meta, and Chinese cloud providers have adopted 380 VDC for select high-density deployments. It requires DC-native server power supplies and custom PDUs. AC distribution remains dominant in mixed-use colocation where tenant equipment is varied, and in Tier IV facilities where independently sourced dual AC paths are architecturally simpler.
Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) from non-linear IT loads increases apparent current drawn from the UPS without increasing real power output. A UPS sized in kVA at unity power factor may be overloaded in amperes when serving loads with high THD, even if kW loading appears within limits. Modern double-conversion UPS with active front-end rectifiers tolerate input THD up to 10% without derating. Legacy thyristor rectifiers require 12-pulse or 18-pulse configurations. Harmonic filters or active power factor correction may be required when THD exceeds 20% to protect transformers and neutral conductors from overheating.
Arc flash incident energy at data center switchgear ranges from 4-40 cal/cm² depending on fault current and clearing time. Primary mitigation methods include: (1) Current-limiting fuses upstream to reduce fault clearing time below 8 ms; (2) Zone-selective interlocking (ZSI) to shorten clearing time at the fault location; (3) Optical arc flash detection systems (light-sensing relays) that trip breakers in under 1 ms; (4) Remote racking systems allowing breakers to be operated with the operator outside the arc flash boundary. NFPA 70E §130.5 mandates an arc flash risk assessment before any energized electrical work at data center voltages.
The single-line diagram (SLD) is the master reference for protection coordination, arc flash analysis, and emergency response. An outdated SLD can misdirect technicians during a power event, cause breaker mis-coordination (larger-than-necessary outages), and produce incorrect arc flash incident energy calculations that expose personnel to injury risk. Any change to the power system — adding a PDU, replacing a breaker, reconfiguring bus ties — must be reflected in the SLD before return to service. Uptime Institute Operations Sustainability Criteria requires SLD accuracy verification as a standing audit checkpoint.
NFPA 70 Article 645 governs IT equipment rooms. Section 645.11 mandates that facilities meeting the §645.2 occupancy criteria (raised floors, controlled access, dedicated HVAC) must have a single disconnect means at each access point to the room to cut all IT equipment and HVAC power. This Emergency Power Off (EPO) must be accessible to first responders and must de-energize all in-room equipment. EPO is separate from normal overcurrent protection and must be integrated into the fire alarm system per NFPA 72. Guarded two-stage EPO switches are required to prevent inadvertent activation — accidental EPO is a leading cause of avoidable data center outages.

      Power-Chain Flow: Utility to IT Rack

      Select any tier to explore typical losses, redundancy options, and component choices at each layer of the power chain.

      Tier 1 Utility Supply Substation · MV cable · ATS · Utility metering ▼
      Typical loss: 0.1–0.3% in cable and metering. Redundancy options: Dual utility feeds from separate substations (Tier IV requirement); single feed with generator backup (Tier II–III).
      • Voltage levels: Utility supply typically at 33 kV, 22 kV, or 11 kV depending on grid region; stepped down at facility substation to 11 kV or 6.6 kV for MV distribution.
      • Automatic Transfer Switches: Main-tie-main switchgear with bus coupling; closed-transition transfer for Tier IV to prevent IT load interruption.
      • Metering and PQ: Revenue metering + power quality analyzer at service entrance; monitors voltage sags, harmonics, and frequency deviations that indicate UPS pre-charge events.
      • Lightning protection: Type 1 SPD at service entrance per NFPA 70 §285; coordinated with Type 2 SPDs at MV/LV transformers.
      Tier 2 Service Entrance & Switchgear MV/LV transformer · Main switchboard · Generators · Bus tie ▼
      Typical loss: 1–2% in transformers. Redundancy options: N+1 transformers on ring bus; bus-tie breaker enabling cross-feed between A and B switchgear sections.
      • Transformers: MV/LV dry-type preferred for indoor use (K-factor rated for non-linear IT loads); liquid-filled permissible with FM-compliant oil containment. Sized at 125% of IT design load minimum.
      • Main switchboard: Draws-out ACB (air circuit breaker) with zone-selective interlocking (ZSI) to minimize arc flash clearing time. Arc flash optical detection supplementation recommended at >25 kA prospective fault current.
      • Generator switchgear: Generator output breakers with under/over-voltage and differential protection; synchronized to bus before closing (closed-transition ATS). Sizing: full IT + cooling + life safety load.
      • Earthing: Low-impedance earthing (LIE) for transformers; TN-S system throughout for IT environments per IEC 60364-5-54.
      Tier 3 UPS System Rectifier · Battery string · Inverter · Static bypass ▼
      Typical loss: 3–4% in double-conversion mode; 1% in eco-mode. Redundancy options: N+1 modular UPS; dual independent UPS strings (A/B) for 2N.
      • Double-conversion topology (VFI): Rectifier converts AC to DC, charges batteries, and feeds inverter simultaneously. Zero transfer time — output is always inverter-sourced regardless of input state.
      • Battery runtime sizing: Minimum 5-minute bridging to generator start; typically 10–15 minutes for operational buffer; 30+ minutes for remote or off-grid sites.
      • Lithium-ion BMS: Cell-level monitoring of voltage, temperature, and state of health; early warning before thermal runaway; faster recharge allows generator off-loading sooner.
      • Eco-mode: 99% efficiency (vs 96-97% double-conversion) but relies on static bypass — brief transfer time on power event. Acceptable only where connected load is tolerant of <20 ms interruptions.
      Tier 4 Power Distribution (PDU / STS) Floor PDU · Static Transfer Switch · RPP · Branch circuits ▼
      Typical loss: 1–2% in PDU transformers and branch wiring. Redundancy options: Dual-corded PDUs (A/B) fed from independent UPS strings; STS for single-corded critical loads requiring source switching.
      • Floor PDU: Step-down isolation transformer (480V → 208/120V in North America; 400/230V in EU); output panel with MCBs for branch circuits. Branch circuit monitoring (BCM) per outlet for capacity management.
      • Static Transfer Switch (STS): Transfers single-corded loads between A and B sources in <4 ms (faster than most IT equipment can detect). Used where dual-corded server PSUs are not available.
      • Rack PDU: Intelligent rPDU with per-outlet metering, remote switching, and environmental monitoring (temperature, humidity sensors). Feed from A and B floor PDUs independently.
      • Power path labeling: A-path circuits in blue, B-path in red — universal color convention that prevents dual-path collapse from miscabling.
      Tier 5 IT Rack / PSU Dual PSU · Power capping · Rack busbar ▼
      Typical loss: 3–8% in server PSU conversion. Redundancy options: Dual PSUs per server, each fed from an independent PDU; rack busbar systems for high-density AI/GPU racks.
      • Dual PSU design: Each server or storage device has two independent power supplies fed from PDU-A and PDU-B respectively. If either PDU path fails, the active PSU continues without interruption.
      • PSU efficiency: 80 PLUS Platinum and Titanium-rated PSUs achieve 92–96% efficiency at 50% load; efficiency drops at low load (<20%). Right-sizing servers to target 50-70% utilization optimizes PSU efficiency.
      • Rack busbar (AI/HPC): For GPU racks exceeding 30 kW, overhead or underfloor busbar systems replace traditional branch circuit wiring; whip connections reduce installation time and support rapid capacity changes.
      • Power capping: Server BMC/iDRAC power capping prevents individual servers from exceeding allocated breaker capacity during thermal or workload events — critical for preventing nuisance tripping on high-density AI infrastructure.

      Power Failure Case Studies

      Three real-world incidents that changed how the industry designs and operates data center power infrastructure.

      March 2021

      OVHcloud SBG2 — Battery Thermal Event, Strasbourg

      A battery thermal event in the UPS room of OVHcloud's SBG2 data center ignited adjacent equipment. Lack of compartmentation between UPS rooms and single-PDU server exposure allowed the fire to propagate, destroying 12,000 servers and rendering three buildings unusable.

      Root cause: UPS battery thermal runaway with no suppression compartmentation; no UPS room fire barrier separating it from the data hall; 30%+ of customer servers had no cold backup (single PDU path).

      Key Takeaways

      • UPS battery rooms must be fire-rated compartments (minimum 1-hr) isolated from the data hall — batteries should never share a room with active IT load.
      • Single-PDU server exposure in a multi-tenant colo collapses customer resilience regardless of building-level redundancy — dual-path PDU A/B is the baseline standard.
      • Li-ion thermal runaway requires dedicated suppression systems (CO₂ or inert gas) compatible with lithium-ion chemistry, not conventional clean agents sized for cable fires.
      June 2019

      Google Cloud us-east1 — Fuel Polishing Failure Cascade

      During an extended utility outage, generators at Google Cloud's us-east1 region began failing under full load due to fuel contamination. What was designed as a 72-hour autonomous power chain was effectively a 48-hour system because diesel stored for over a year had accumulated bacterial growth and water contamination that blocked injectors under sustained load.

      Root cause: Fuel polishing program had lapsed due to a vendor change; annual fuel sample testing had been deferred; contamination was not detectable in no-load weekly tests but appeared catastrophically under full sustained load.

      Key Takeaways

      • 72-hour autonomy is a calculation, not a guarantee — fuel quality must be verified by quarterly sample analysis and continuous polishing, not assumed from tank volume alone.
      • Generator full-load tests (NFPA 110 annual 4-hour test) are not sufficient to catch slow-developing fuel contamination; fuel sample analysis is the only reliable indicator.
      • Vendor transitions for fuel or maintenance contracts must trigger immediate inspection — continuity of maintenance data does not pass between contractors automatically.
      December 2021

      AWS US-East-1 — Power System Controller Firmware Bug

      A firmware defect in AWS's automated power system controllers caused them to interpret normal operating conditions as fault states, triggering automated protective actions that took down power systems across multiple Availability Zones simultaneously. The automation designed to protect the power infrastructure became the failure mechanism, knocking out EC2, RDS, EFS, and dozens of dependent services for over 7 hours.

      Root cause: Firmware update to power system controllers introduced a logic error in fault classification; the update had not been tested in a live-load environment that could replicate the specific operating state that triggered the bug.

      Key Takeaways

      • Power infrastructure firmware and control software must be subject to the same change management, staged rollout, and regression testing discipline as application software — "infrastructure is stable" is not an excuse for skipping test protocols.
      • Automated protective systems need manual override capability that is tested regularly; if automation itself fails, operators must be able to revert to manual control within seconds, not minutes.
      • Multi-AZ architecture is not sufficient protection when the failure mechanism is shared power infrastructure automation — true blast radius isolation requires independent control plane stacks per AZ, not just independent UPS and generators.

      Power Infrastructure Glossary

      14 essential terms for data center power and electrical engineers.

      UPS
      Uninterruptible Power Supply. Device that provides continuous power by switching to battery (or flywheel) on utility failure, bridging the gap until generator pick-up. VFI (double-conversion) class provides zero transfer time per IEC 62040-3.
      PDU
      Power Distribution Unit. Transforms and distributes power from the UPS to server racks. Floor PDUs step down voltage (480→208 V or 400→230 V) and include isolation transformers; rack PDUs distribute to individual outlets with per-port metering.
      ATS
      Automatic Transfer Switch. Detects source failure and switches load from normal to alternate source. Open-transition ATS has a brief interruption (100–200 ms); closed-transition parallels both sources momentarily for zero interruption.
      MV/LV Switchgear
      Medium Voltage (above 1 kV, typically 6.6–33 kV) and Low Voltage (below 1 kV) switching and protection assemblies. MV switchgear controls utility feeds and generator outputs; LV switchgear distributes to downstream UPS and PDU panels.
      Double-Conversion
      UPS topology (IEC VFI class) where input AC is converted to DC and back to AC continuously. IT load is always supplied from the inverter — zero transfer time, complete power conditioning, and full isolation from grid anomalies.
      Line-Interactive
      UPS topology (IEC VI class) where the inverter conditions output and charges batteries but the load is supplied from the AC input through a voltage regulator. Brief transfer time (2–4 ms) to battery during deep sags or outages. Lower cost but less isolation than double-conversion.
      Eco-Mode
      UPS operating mode where load is supplied from utility input (bypass path) at 99% efficiency, with the inverter on hot standby. Transfers to inverter in <1 ms on utility anomaly. Trades a small transfer risk for significant energy savings; accepted in some Tier III designs but typically avoided in Tier IV.
      BESS
      Battery Energy Storage System. Grid-scale or behind-the-meter lithium-ion battery array that can replace or supplement diesel generators for power chain resilience and demand charge reduction. Increasingly integrated into hyperscale data centers for sustainability targets and generator-less operation for short outages.
      kAIC
      Kiloampere Interrupting Capacity. The maximum fault current a circuit breaker or fuse can safely interrupt without damage. Data center switchgear near a utility substation may see 50–100 kA prospective fault current — all protective devices must be rated at or above this value.
      Arc Flash
      Explosive release of energy from an electrical fault, producing plasma temperatures up to 20,000 °C and incident energy measured in cal/cm². NFPA 70E requires a risk assessment before any energized electrical work; mitigation includes current-limiting fuses, zone-selective interlocking, optical detection, and remote racking.
      THD
      Total Harmonic Distortion. The ratio of all harmonic current components to the fundamental frequency current, expressed as a percentage. Non-linear IT loads (switched-mode power supplies) generate high THD that overheats neutral conductors, transformers, and UPS rectifiers. Active front-end (AFE) UPS rectifiers and harmonic filters mitigate THD.
      Power Factor
      Ratio of real power (kW) to apparent power (kVA). Modern IT equipment typically operates at PF 0.95–0.99. UPS systems are often rated in kVA — a 1000 kVA UPS at PF 0.9 supports 900 kW of real load. Poor PF from reactive loads wastes cable and transformer capacity without delivering useful work.
      IDC
      Internet Data Centre. Facility providing outsourced IT infrastructure (servers, storage, networking) to multiple tenants. Power infrastructure in IDCs must support diverse tenant equipment with unknown load characteristics, making PF tolerance, harmonic management, and flexible redundancy architectures especially important.
      EPO
      Emergency Power Off. NFPA 70 §645.11 mandated disconnect system that de-energizes all IT equipment from a single control point at each room access. Required for first-responder safety during electrical fires. Guarded two-stage actuation prevents accidental activation — inadvertent EPO is a leading cause of avoidable data center outages.
