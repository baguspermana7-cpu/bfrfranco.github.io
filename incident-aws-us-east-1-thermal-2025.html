AWS US-EAST-1 Cooling/Thermal Event and use1-az4 Power Loss

Amazon Web Services 2026-05-07 20h 25m core impact CoolingPower

On 7 May 2026 cooling systems failed in AWS's use1-az4 availability zone in US-EAST-1 (Northern Virginia). As data-hall temperatures crossed operating thresholds, servers automatically shut down to protect hardware, cutting power to the EC2 instances and EBS volumes hosted on that gear. The compute and storage loss cascaded to dependent services — IoT Core, ELB, NAT Gateway and Redshift — with elevated error rates and slower provisioning. AWS shifted traffic off the impaired zone and advised customers to relaunch or redistribute workloads in other US-EAST-1 availability zones. AWS first flagged the problem at 5:25 PM PDT on 7 May and restored cooling to pre-incident capacity at 1:50 PM PDT on 8 May, roughly 20 hours 25 minutes later. Downstream, Coinbase reported a multi-hour outage in which users could not trade or move funds. No formal AWS post-incident report was published in the available sources.

Root cause

On the evening of Thursday, 2026-05-07 (PDT), a cooling-system failure occurred in a single AWS data center serving the use1-az4 Availability Zone of the US-EAST-1 region (Northern Virginia). Per press coverage of AWS's own Health Dashboard, "AWS confirmed the outage was due to overheating caused by a cooling failure at its North Virginia data center, US-East-1, which is a heavily used region" (CRN). This "cooling failure" is the highest-level cause AWS disclosed; the specific reason the cooling plant failed (mechanical, controls/BMS, coolant-loop, or power-to-cooling) was never publicly explained, so the authoritative causal record stops at "cooling failure." The mechanism that turned lost cooling into a service outage was thermal-protection automation. As temperatures climbed past design limits, "servers automatically shut down when the temperatures exceeded the operating thresholds in order to protect the hardware" (Network World). That automated shutdown is the most likely source of the "loss of power" AWS reported: "EC2 instances and EBS volumes hosted on impacted hardware are affected by the loss of power during the thermal event" in the use1-az4 Availability Zone (Network World). AWS's wording ties the power loss to the thermal event rather than to a utility, UPS, or generator fault; on that basis this incident is best read as a thermal event whose "power loss" was a protective de-energization of servers, not a primary electrical-supply failure. AWS did not, however, explicitly rule out an electrical fault, so the "protective shutdown, not electrical" reading is a well-supported interpretation, not an AWS-stated fact. Impact then propagated along AWS's service dependency graph. AWS's first post came at 5:25 PM PDT; by 6:47 PM PDT it warned that "Other AWS services that depend on the affected EC2 instances and EBS volumes in this Availability Zone may also experience impairments" (Network World). Network World names IoT Core, ELB, NAT Gateway, and Redshift as affected; CRN additionally reports SageMaker, ElastiCache, and Amazon EKS. Recovery was protracted: at 8:06 PM PDT AWS said "progress is slower than originally anticipated," and at 10:11 PM PDT it reported only "incremental progress to restore cooling systems," with services "experiencing elevated error rates and latencies for some workflows." Power was restored to the AZ "several hours after the thermal event occurred" (CRN), but full cooling capacity was not restored to pre-incident levels until 1:50 PM PDT Friday 2026-05-08 — roughly 20 hours after the first report. Coinbase was disrupted for about seven hours (CRN). Important caveat: no AWS official post-incident review (PIR/RFO/aws.amazon.com/message) was located. The entire causal record rests on two press summaries of Health Dashboard updates, so the disclosed cause is "cooling failure" with the underlying failure mode and the state of cooling redundancy both undisclosed.

Contributing factors

  • Undisclosed underlying cooling-plant failure mode: AWS attributed the event to a 'cooling failure' but never published the physical root cause beneath it (mechanical, controls/BMS, coolant, or power-to-cooling). The true first cause is uncharacterized in the public record.
  • Cooling redundancy did not arrest the temperature rise: rack temperatures exceeded operating thresholds, so whatever cooling capacity existed did not hold the envelope. Note this is inference — the sources contain no positive detail on use1-az4 cooling-redundancy design or why it did not engage; AWS disclosed nothing on this point.
  • Concentration of load on a single AZ within a heavily used region: CRN describes US-EAST-1 as 'a heavily used region,' so a single-AZ thermal fault translated into broad, high-visibility impact across many customers and dependent services.
  • Deep service-dependency chains on the affected EC2/EBS hardware: building-block services (IoT Core, ELB, NAT Gateway, Redshift per Network World; SageMaker, ElastiCache, EKS per CRN) inherited the failure once the underlying compute/storage lost power, amplifying a localized hardware outage into a multi-service impairment.
  • Slow, difficult recovery: AWS signaled recovery was harder than expected ('progress is slower than originally anticipated'; only 'incremental progress to restore cooling systems'), and full cooling capacity took roughly 20 hours to restore, extending impact well past the earlier power restoration.
  • Absence of a formal post-incident review: no PIR/RFO/aws.amazon.com/message document was found, leaving the causal record dependent on brief Health Dashboard status updates relayed by press rather than an AWS engineering analysis — a transparency/learning gap.

Lessons learnt

  • A hardware-safety feature can be an availability failure mode: thermal auto-shutdown correctly protects servers, but when it fires hall-wide it turns a cooling fault into simultaneous power loss for every instance on that gear.
  • Foundational-service blast radius is real: because EC2 and EBS underpin higher-level services, their impairment cascaded to IoT Core, ELB, NAT Gateway and Redshift instead of remaining a localized power event.
  • Single-AZ concentration breaks quorum systems: Coinbase's report of losing 3 of 5 quorum nodes — all in one cluster placement group — shows that placing a majority of a consensus group where one AZ's failure can take them out defeats the point of the quorum.
  • Recovery is serialized behind cooling: racks could not safely return until temperatures were restored, so the environmental fault set the floor on total downtime (~20 hours).
  • Disclosure gaps hamper learning: with no formal AWS COE and only qualitative Coinbase impact, neither plant-level fault detail nor quantified customer impact is available to drive precise remediation.

Improvements & remediation

  • Distribute quorum-critical and consensus nodes across multiple availability zones so no single AZ — or single cluster placement group — holds a voting majority.
  • Architect stateful services (matching engine, event streaming, order routing) for cross-zone failover and rapid quorum re-establishment.
  • Pre-stage multi-AZ relaunch runbooks (and, where applicable, snapshot-based restore) so customers can execute the zone-redistribution recovery path within minutes, not hours.
  • Expand disaster-recovery testing to explicitly include single-AZ thermal/power-loss scenarios rather than only instance- or service-level failures.
  • Improve environmental resilience and redundancy in the cooling plant and instrument restoration progress at rack granularity to shorten the cooling-first recovery serialization.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism

The incident began as an environmental fault: cooling systems failed in use1-az4. As the data hall heated past operating thresholds, servers executed their designed thermal-protection response and powered themselves off to avoid heat damage. That autonomous shutdown is the pivot point of the whole event — it converted a cooling problem into a simultaneous, correlated power loss across the EC2 instances and EBS volumes hosted on the affected hardware. Both Network World and The Register independently describe this power-loss-during-thermal-event mechanism, giving the root cause two-source corroboration.

Why it cascaded

EC2 and EBS are not leaf services; they are the compute and block-storage substrate on which much of AWS is built. When they lost power, the impairment radiated outward to IoT Core, ELB, NAT Gateway and Redshift, and surfaced to customers as slower provisioning, elevated error rates and higher latencies. The cascade continued beyond AWS: Coinbase's Raft-based matching engine, Kafka streaming and order-routing services were disrupted, and — by Coinbase's own account — three of five quorum nodes were lost, enough to break consensus and halt trading. The event is therefore a textbook demonstration of how a single-hall physical fault propagates up a dependency stack into a customer-facing financial outage.

Recovery

Recovery was gated by the environment. AWS confirmed the use1-az4 problem at 5:25 PM PDT on 7 May, warned of cascading service impairments at 6:47 PM, reported cooling restoration progressing 'slower than anticipated' by ~8:06 PM, brought additional cooling capacity online with some racks recovering by 10:11 PM, published a fuller explanation at 6:51 AM PDT on 8 May, and restored cooling to pre-incident capacity at 1:50 PM PDT — about 20 hours 25 minutes after onset. Racks could only return once temperatures were safe again, so cooling restoration was the critical path. In parallel, AWS shifted traffic off the impaired zone and steered customers toward other US-EAST-1 zones. Coinbase's own recovery took much of the following day via a staged cancel-only, then auction, then normal re-enable sequence.

Organizational and systemic factors

Two structural choices shaped the impact. On the AWS side, the concentration of workload in US-EAST-1 means any hall-level fault there has amplified consequences — this is the same region behind a ~15-hour, 70-plus-service outage in October 2025. On the customer side, Coinbase's loss of a quorum majority within the affected zone, with all five Raft nodes in a single cluster placement group, indicates consensus-critical components were not spread widely enough across availability zones to survive a single-AZ loss. Coinbase's four remediation items — cross-zone recovery, better quorum restoration, multi-AZ messaging and expanded DR testing — are all aimed at that gap, though the source reports them as committed action items without owners, deadlines or verification.

Cross-incident pattern

The May 2026 thermal event extends a recurring 'notorious US-EAST-1' pattern. The immediately preceding major outage (19-20 October 2025) ran roughly 15 hours and cascaded across more than 70 AWS services after a DynamoDB DNS race condition; the May event is shorter in service breadth but comparable in duration and again triggered by an infrastructure fault localized to the region. The through-line is not a single repeated root cause but a repeated topology: heavy dependence on one region, foundational services with wide blast radius, and downstream customers whose own architectures assume more isolation than a correlated AZ-level failure actually provides.

Technical deep-dive

The failure chain is a thermal-loss-to-protective-shutdown sequence in a data hall. Under normal operation the use1-az4 cooling system removes heat dissipated by dense racks of EC2/EBS host hardware, holding component temperatures within a design envelope. When cooling failed on 2026-05-07, heat removal fell below heat generation and rack temperatures rose. Server firmware/BMC thermal-protection logic continuously compares inlet, CPU, and component sensors against configured operating thresholds; once those thresholds were crossed the logic triggered automatic shutdown: per AWS, "servers automatically shut down when the temperatures exceeded the operating thresholds in order to protect the hardware" (Network World). This is a deliberate hardware-preserving trade — the platform sheds its own power rather than risk sustained over-temperature damage. Because the compute/storage substrate lost power, AWS characterized the customer-facing effect as a "loss of power during the thermal event" affecting hosted EC2 instances and EBS volumes in use1-az4 (Network World). Read against AWS's wording, this "power loss" is best interpreted as a protective de-energization of servers tied to the thermal event rather than a failure of the electrical distribution (utility, UPS, generators); AWS did not explicitly confirm or exclude an electrical fault, so this remains an interpretation rather than a disclosed fact. The blast radius then propagated along AWS's dependency graph: managed services running data or control paths on EC2/EBS in that AZ — IoT Core, ELB, NAT Gateway, and Redshift (Network World), plus SageMaker, ElastiCache, and EKS (CRN) — began "experiencing elevated error rates and latencies for some workflows" (Network World). Recovery required restoring physical cooling capacity before shut-down hardware could be safely brought back and kept online, which is why power returned to the AZ "several hours after the thermal event" (CRN) while full cooling capacity was not restored to pre-incident levels until 1:50 PM PDT the next day (~20 hours from the first 5:25 PM PDT report, an approximate elapsed-time inference). The public technical record does not resolve the mechanism beneath the cooling failure, nor whether/why cooling redundancy failed to prevent the threshold breach — both remain undisclosed by AWS, and no official post-incident review was published.
