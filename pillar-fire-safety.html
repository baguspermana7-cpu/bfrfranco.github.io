Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about data center fire protection and life safety.

Clean agents like FM-200 and Novec 1230 suppress fires by chemical interruption or heat absorption without leaving residue or causing water damage to servers, storage, and networking equipment. They discharge in under 10 seconds and can extinguish Class A, B, and C fires while equipment continues running. Water-based systems risk catastrophic damage to electronics through short circuits, corrosion, and extended downtime during cleanup and hardware replacement.
An Emergency Power Off (EPO) button immediately de-energizes all electrical equipment in a data center zone. Required by NFPA 70 and local fire codes, it enables first responders to safely enter during emergencies. EPO should only be activated when there is an imminent threat to human life, such as an uncontrolled electrical fire or electrocution risk. Accidental EPO activation is a leading cause of data center outages, so modern facilities use guarded two-stage switches and conduct regular staff awareness training to prevent inadvertent shutdowns.
Yes, in most jurisdictions. Building codes and insurance requirements typically mandate sprinkler coverage regardless of clean agent systems. The standard approach uses pre-action sprinklers with double interlock, requiring both a detection signal and physical heat activation of the sprinkler head before water flows. This design minimizes accidental discharge. Clean agents protect against small, fast-growing electrical fires at the rack level, while sprinklers provide building-level protection for larger fire events that overwhelm or exhaust the clean agent supply.
Halon 1301 was phased out under the Montreal Protocol due to its extreme ozone-depleting potential (ODP of 10 — roughly ten times more damaging than CFC-11). Production ceased in developed nations by 1994. The primary replacements are HFC-227ea (FM-200, GWP 3,220, zero ODP) and FK-5-1-12 (Novec 1230, GWP 1, zero ODP). Inert gas blends — IG-541 (Inergen) and IG-100 (nitrogen) — have both zero ODP and zero GWP, though they require larger cylinder volumes. Existing Halon systems may still operate on recycled agent stocks, but new installation is prohibited in most countries.
Design concentration is the minimum volumetric percentage of clean agent in air required to extinguish a fire of a specific class, as defined by NFPA 2001 §5.3. For FM-200, the minimum is 7.0% v/v on Class A surface fires; for Novec 1230 it is 4.2% v/v. NFPA 2001 requires this concentration to be reached within 10 seconds of activation. Under-concentration allows fire to survive the discharge; over-concentration wastes agent and may exceed the NOAEL safety limit in occupied spaces. Design concentration is calculated using the Halon Alternatives Research Corporation (HARC) formula, factoring in room volume, minimum temperature, and enclosure leakage.
Aspirating smoke detectors (VESDA) actively draw air samples through a network of small-bore sampling pipes into a central laser nephelometer, capable of detecting smoke at obscuration levels below 0.001% per foot — hours before visible smoke develops. Spot detectors are passive: smoke must physically drift into the detector housing and exceed a threshold in the ionization or photoelectric chamber. In high-airflow data center environments, cold-aisle containment and pressurized hot aisles can dilute or redirect smoke away from spot detector heads, significantly delaying alarm. VESDA is strongly preferred for below-floor and above-ceiling plenum coverage, where air movement is least predictable.
NFPA 2001 §7.1 recommends enclosure integrity (door fan) testing at commissioning and at least every 12 months thereafter. The test pressurizes the protected room to measure leakage and calculate the agent hold time — how long concentration remains above the design level post-discharge. A minimum 10-minute hold time is required to allow detection, suppression confirmation, and safe re-entry by trained personnel. If the room fails this test, sealing remediation is required before the system is returned to service. Many insurers and Authorities Having Jurisdiction (AHJs) independently mandate annual certification as a condition of coverage.
IG-100 is pure nitrogen. IG-541 (Inergen) is a blend of 52% nitrogen, 40% argon, and 8% CO₂. Both suppress fire by reducing oxygen concentration from the ambient 21% to below the 15% threshold required for combustion, while keeping it above the approximately 12% human survival level. The CO₂ component in IG-541 stimulates deeper breathing, helping occupants hyperventilate and exit faster during discharge — a meaningful safety benefit when egress takes more than 30 seconds. IG-100 is generally lower cost and simpler to source globally. Both require 40–60% more cylinder volume than equivalent halocarbon protection and must be stored at 200–300 bar.
NOAEL (No Observed Adverse Effect Level) is the relevant safety threshold for occupied spaces. For FM-200, the NOAEL is 9.0% v/v; for Novec 1230 it is 10.0% v/v. Since design concentrations (7.0% and 4.2% respectively) are comfortably below these limits, both agents can discharge into normally occupied rooms provided egress is not impaired. LOAEL (Lowest Observed Adverse Effect Level) is the concentration at which physiological effects first appear — for halocarbons, this is typically cardiac sensitization sensitizing the heart to epinephrine-induced arrhythmias. NFPA 2001 §1.6.1 explicitly prohibits designing above NOAEL in occupied spaces without special engineering justification and AHJ approval.
EU Regulation 2024/573 phases down HFCs based on CO₂-equivalent quota. FM-200 (HFC-227ea, GWP 3,220) sits in the highest phase-down bracket. From 2024, new systems in the EU face increasingly restricted quota allocation, with a near-total restriction on high-GWP HFC use in new fire suppression applications anticipated by 2032–2033. Existing installed systems are not retroactively banned, but refill agent availability will tighten significantly through the decade. Operators specifying new EU data center suppression systems should default to Novec 1230 (GWP 1) or inert gas. UK post-Brexit F-gas regulations mirror the EU trajectory with minor timing differences.
NFPA 13 classifies data centers as Ordinary Hazard Group 1 or 2 based on combustible loading. Minimum design density for OH-1 is 0.15 gpm/ft² over the most hydraulically remote 1,500 ft². NFPA 75 §8.3 additionally requires sprinklers in sub-floor raised-floor cavities and above-ceiling plenums when cable loading exceeds defined limits. Pre-action systems must meet the same hydraulic density as wet systems — the pre-action valve simply holds water out of the pipes until two independent detection events confirm a fire. For high-density AI/GPU racks exceeding 30 kW per rack, FM Global 5-32 recommends enhanced density calculations and in-rack suppression supplementation.
Yes, but it requires a full electrical engineering study to map all power feed circuits, UPS outputs, generator feed paths, and transfer switch connections that the EPO relay must capture. NFPA 70 §645.11 mandates EPO for facilities meeting §645.2 occupancy criteria (IT rooms over 232 m² / 2,500 ft² or with raised-floor access). Retrofit projects typically add shunt-trip breakers at main switchboards, install guarded two-stage push-button stations at all primary exits, and integrate the relay output into the fire alarm control panel. Commissioning requires a live-load test verifying full de-energization sequence and written documentation filed with the local AHJ. Budget 6–18 months for permitting and construction in live facilities.
A double-interlock pre-action system requires two simultaneous, independent events before water enters the dry piping: (1) the fire detection system must signal (smoke, heat, or flame detector activates), AND (2) a sprinkler head must physically open from direct heat exposure. Only when both conditions occur concurrently does the pre-action valve release water. This design makes accidental discharge nearly impossible — a single spurious detector alarm or a mechanically damaged sprinkler head cannot alone flood the room. NFPA 13 §8.2 governs valve type, supervisory air pressure requirements, and detection circuit zoning. Double-interlock is the universal standard for IT rooms containing sensitive electronic equipment.
NFPA 2001 clean-agent suppression combined with double-interlock pre-action and VESDA detection typically earns 35–55% reductions in property insurance premiums versus water-only sprinkler facilities, per FM Global and major commercial underwriters. The combination significantly reduces the probable maximum loss (PML) estimate. Additional premium credits apply for annual door fan integrity testing records, third-party commissioning verification, documented annual inspection per NFPA 25, and FM Global Approval of the system design. Some insurers also offer credits for in-rack suppression at high-density AI/GPU installations and for UPS thermal runaway detection systems integrated with the fire alarm.
AI/GPU racks exceeding 30 kW per rack introduce fire risks that standard IT room design does not fully address. FM Global 5-32 (2021 edition) specifically addresses high-density computing environments. Key adaptations include: (1) In-rack suppression supplementation alongside room-level clean agent — targeting ignition at the source before spread; (2) IR or gas-sensor thermal runaway detection for GPU module battery banks and capacitor banks, which can trigger before VESDA detects products of combustion; (3) Enhanced sprinkler density calculation accounting for higher fuel load; (4) Increased clean-agent design concentration verification as high-airflow cooling (rear-door heat exchangers, liquid cooling manifolds) can increase enclosure leakage. Liquid-cooled deployments (direct liquid, immersion) present different fire profiles — reduced ignition frequency but concentrated coolant leak risk requiring different detection strategies.

      5-Tier Layered Defense Model

      Select any tier to explore the technologies, standards, and controls at each layer of the defense-in-depth strategy.

      Tier 1 Prevention Compartmentation · Hot work permits · Fuel reduction · Housekeeping ▼
      Goal: Eliminate ignition sources and fuel loads before a fire can start.
      • Compartmentation: 1-hr fire-rated walls between IT rooms and other occupancies per NFPA 75 §9; 2-hr separation for large facilities per FM Global 5-32.
      • Hot work control: Permit system for welding, grinding, and open flame; 30-minute fire watch post-completion per NFPA 51B.
      • Combustible loading: No cardboard boxes, packing materials, or unnecessary combustibles permitted inside IT rooms; cable quantity minimized per NFPA 75 §8.4.
      • Electrical maintenance: Thermographic scanning of switchgear annually; loose connections re-torqued per NFPA 70B §11.14.
      Tier 2 Detection VESDA aspirating · Spot smoke · Beam · Thermal/IR camera ▼
      Goal: Identify incipient fires within seconds, triggering alarm and suppression before flames develop.
      • VESDA: Sensitivity to 0.001% obscuration/ft; detects smoldering cables hours before flames per NFPA 72 §17.7.
      • Spot detectors: Photoelectric (preferred over ionization in IT environments) on 9 m maximum spacing; positioned to catch stratification patterns.
      • Linear beam: Single or double-ended beam detectors for wide-span areas; tested annually with calibrated filter kit.
      • Thermal/IR: Continuous IR camera monitoring of battery banks and high-density GPU racks increasingly standard for hyperscale facilities.
      Tier 3 Suppression Clean agent discharge · Pre-action sprinkler · In-rack suppression ▼
      Goal: Extinguish the fire within 10 seconds, protecting equipment while avoiding water damage.
      • Clean agent (primary): FM-200 or Novec 1230 reaches design concentration in <10 s; zero residue, leaves equipment operational after discharge and ventilation.
      • Pre-action sprinkler (backup): Double-interlock system provides building-level protection; water only flows on dual confirmed event — prevents accidental flooding.
      • In-rack suppression: FM Global 5-32 recommends supplemental in-rack systems for racks exceeding 30 kW (GPU/AI infrastructure); targets localized ignition before spread.
      Tier 4 Containment Smoke dampers · Fire doors · EPO · Aisle containment ▼
      Goal: Limit fire and smoke spread to the zone of origin; protect adjacent spaces and personnel.
      • Smoke dampers: HVAC smoke/fire dampers activate on detection to prevent smoke migration through air pathways per NFPA 90A §6.5.
      • Fire doors: Self-closing, rated fire doors at all IT room entries; door holders released on fire alarm signal.
      • EPO: Emergency power-off de-energizes all IT equipment to prevent fire propagation through electrical faults; guarded two-stage activation per NFPA 70 §645.11.
      • Aisle containment: Physical containment structures limit oxygen supply and reduce fire spread rate within hot or cold aisles.
      Tier 5 Response Alarm system · Evacuation · FD dispatch · Post-incident recovery ▼
      Goal: Ensure safe evacuation, fire department response, and structured recovery to minimize business impact.
      • Alarm notification: Audible and visual notification appliances throughout occupancy per NFPA 72 §18; fire alarm monitoring by UL-listed central station 24/7.
      • Evacuation: Pre-scripted evacuation procedures with muster points, accountability system, and designated fire warden per NFPA 101 §4.8.
      • FD coordination: Knox box key access; pre-incident planning document filed with local fire department covering hazardous materials (clean agent cylinders) and utility shutoff locations.
      • Recovery: Post-incident damage assessment protocol; documented restoration procedure including agent recharge, integrity re-test, and insurer notification within 24 hours.

      Incident Case Studies

      Documented data center fire events — what happened, why, and what the industry learned.

      2021-03-10 — Strasbourg, France

      OVHcloud SBG2 Datacenter Fire

      A catastrophic fire destroyed OVHcloud's SBG2 data center and severely damaged the adjacent SBG1 building, taking both facilities offline. An estimated 12,000 physical servers were lost, affecting more than 3.6 million websites and services worldwide.

      Root cause:
      UPS thermal runaway in an unsprinklered UPS room. A lithium-iron-phosphate battery module entered thermal runaway; the energy released overwhelmed the local suppression capacity and spread to adjacent cable trays. SBG2 had no compartmentation between the UPS room and main data hall — a single protected zone failure cascaded building-wide.

      Key Takeaways

      • UPS rooms must be physically compartmented from the IT hall with rated fire walls and independent suppression zones.
      • Battery thermal runaway requires dedicated early-warning detection (IR, gas sensor) — smoke detectors alone react too late for LiFePO4 events.
      • Business continuity planning must account for total-loss facility scenarios, not just partial outages; geographic redundancy proved decisive for SBG2 clients.
      2023-04-02 — Ogden, Utah, USA

      WebNX Generator Fire & Roof Penetration

      A fire originating in a diesel generator set spread to the roof structure of the WebNX data center, causing significant structural damage and forcing emergency evacuation. While customer equipment survived, the facility sustained weeks of downtime for structural repairs and re-commissioning.

      Root cause:
      Generator exhaust piping overheated adjacent combustible roofing materials at a penetration point. The generator room lacked a listed fire-rated penetration seal, and the roofing material did not meet the non-combustible classification required at the penetration. A day-tank fuel spill during refueling contributed accelerant to the initial ignition.

      Key Takeaways

      • Generator exhaust penetrations through rated assemblies must use listed fire-resistive collars or sealants — generic firestopping is insufficient for hot exhaust pathways.
      • Day-tank fuel management procedures must include spill containment; fueling should never occur during live generator operation.
      • Generator rooms are a leading fire origin in colocation facilities — they require dedicated suppression (typically water mist or CO₂), not just clean-agent protection scoped to the IT hall.
      Near-miss composite — Multiple facilities

      VESDA Early Warning: Hyperscale Near-Miss

      At a major hyperscale operator's EU campus, a VESDA alert triggered at 03:14 local time, registering 0.004% obscuration in a cold aisle — well below any visible smoke threshold. Operations responded within 4 minutes and located a cable jacket on a 48V busbar beginning to pyrolyze. No fire developed; the busbar was isolated and replaced within 6 hours with zero downtime.

      Root cause:
      A busbar conductor connection had been under-torqued during a maintenance event 11 months prior. Progressive resistance heating reached the insulation degradation threshold overnight during a high-load period. Spot detectors in the aisle showed no alarm; the VESDA sampling pipe located 60 cm above the busbar tray captured the early pyrolysis products.

      Key Takeaways

      • VESDA's sub-0.01% obscuration sensitivity enables intervention hours before a spot detector would alarm — the difference between a 6-hour maintenance event and a total loss.
      • Torque records for high-current connections must be verified after every maintenance event; re-torque programs should run every 24 months per NFPA 70B §11.14.
      • AI-driven VESDA trend analysis (rising baseline concentration over weeks) can predict failure days before an acute event — a growing capability at hyperscale facilities.

      Inline Glossary

      Quick-reference definitions for the key terms used across fire safety standards and system design.

      NFPA
      National Fire Protection Association — US-based standards body publishing the 300+ NFPA codes and standards (including NFPA 72, 75, 76, 101, 2001) that form the legal and engineering baseline for fire protection globally.
      VESDA
      Very Early Smoke Detection Apparatus — an aspirating smoke detector system that actively draws air samples through pipe networks into a laser nephelometer, detecting smoke at ≤0.001% obscuration per foot — orders of magnitude more sensitive than spot detectors.
      EPO
      Emergency Power Off — a guarded pushbutton switch (NFPA 70 §645.11) that immediately de-energizes all electrical circuits in an IT room. Intended solely for life-safety emergencies; accidental activation is a leading cause of data center outages.
      FM-200
      Trade name for HFC-227ea (heptafluoropropane). A colorless, odorless gas that suppresses fire by interrupting chemical chain reactions. Zero ODP, GWP of 3,220. Discharges in <10 s at 7.0% v/v design concentration. Faces EU F-gas regulatory restrictions.
      Novec 1230
      3M trade name for FK-5-1-12 (dodecafluoro-2-methylpentan-3-one). A liquid-stored, low-pressure clean agent with GWP of 1 and zero ODP. Design concentration 4.2% v/v. 3M exited the PFAS chemical space in 2025; alternate-brand equivalents maintain the FK-5-1-12 chemistry.
      ODP
      Ozone Depletion Potential — a relative index comparing a substance's ability to destroy stratospheric ozone versus CFC-11 (reference = 1.0). Halon 1301 ODP = 10; FM-200 and Novec 1230 have ODP = 0. Substances with ODP >0 are banned under the Montreal Protocol.
      GWP
      Global Warming Potential — a measure of how much heat a greenhouse gas traps in the atmosphere over 100 years relative to CO₂ (GWP = 1). FM-200 GWP = 3,220; Novec 1230 GWP = 1; IG-541 and IG-100 GWP = 0.
      IG-541
      Inergen — an inert gas blend of 52% N₂, 40% Ar, and 8% CO₂. Suppresses fire by oxygen dilution; GWP = 0; ODP = 0. The CO₂ component stimulates faster respiration for occupant egress. Requires larger cylinder banks than halocarbons.
      IG-100
      Pure nitrogen used as an inert fire suppressant. GWP = 0; ODP = 0; lowest cost inert gas option. Suppresses by reducing oxygen below 15% combustion threshold. No CO₂ respiratory stimulant benefit vs. IG-541.
      Halocarbon
      A class of chemical compounds containing carbon bonded to one or more halogens (fluorine, chlorine, bromine). Fire suppression halocarbons include FM-200 (HFC), Novec 1230 (FK), and the now-banned Halons. They suppress fire chemically by interrupting radical chain reactions.
      Design Concentration
      The minimum volumetric percentage of clean agent in air required to extinguish a fire of a given class, per NFPA 2001 §5.3. FM-200: 7.0% v/v (Class A); Novec 1230: 4.2% v/v. Must be achieved within 10 seconds and must not exceed the agent's NOAEL in occupied spaces.
      NOAEL
      No Observed Adverse Effect Level — the maximum clean agent concentration at which no adverse physiological effects are observed in humans. FM-200 NOAEL: 9.0% v/v; Novec 1230 NOAEL: 10.0% v/v. Design concentrations must stay below NOAEL for occupied spaces per NFPA 2001 §1.6.
      LOAEL
      Lowest Observed Adverse Effect Level — the lowest concentration at which adverse effects appear in humans. For halocarbons, this is typically cardiac sensitization (arrhythmia risk under epinephrine exposure). LOAEL for FM-200: 10.5% v/v. NFPA 2001 prohibits designing above NOAEL in occupied areas.
      Pre-Action Sprinkler
      A sprinkler system in which the supply pipe remains dry until one (single-interlock) or two (double-interlock) independent detection events confirm a fire. Double-interlock is standard in IT rooms: both a detector signal AND a sprinkler head open are required before water flows, preventing accidental flooding.
