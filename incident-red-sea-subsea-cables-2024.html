Red Sea Subsea Cable Cuts — Rubymar Anchor Severs AAE-1, Seacom/TGN-EA and EIG off Yemen

AAE-1 / EIG / Seacom / Tata (TGN) consortia 2024-02-24 3408h 0m core impact NetworkSupply chain

On 24 February 2024 three submarine cable systems — Seacom (which Kentik groups with TGN-EA), AAE-1 and EIG — were severed off the west coast of Yemen in the Red Sea. The generally-accepted cause is the anchor of the MV Rubymar, an abandoned cargo ship struck by Houthi forces on 18 February, dragging across the cable corridor after the anchor was dropped when the vessel came under fire. The cuts degraded a large share of Asia-Europe-Middle-East transit (commonly cited at ~25%, though that figure is unattributed in reachable sources) and were felt across East Africa and the Middle East. Physical repair did not begin until July 2024, when an E-Marine cable ship restored AAE-1 first and then the remaining two cables. The cause is the accepted narrative rather than a forensic consortium finding; TGN-EA is named alongside Seacom by Kentik, but whether it is a physically distinct 4th cable or shares the Seacom system remains only partly corroborated.

Root cause

TRIGGER. On 18 February 2024, Houthi forces launched two anti-ship missiles at the Belize-flagged, UK-owned cargo ship MV Rubymar in the Red Sea near Yemen, one of which struck it. The crew dropped anchor and abandoned the crippled ship, which then began to drift while dragging its deployed anchor across the seabed — "one of the top causes of submarine cable cuts" according to the International Cable Protection Committee (as cited by Kentik). The Rubymar sank on 2 March 2024. MECHANISM. On the morning of Saturday 24 February 2024, network telemetry (Kentik) recorded two disruption clusters about five minutes apart: Seacom/TGN-EA failed at 09:46 UTC and AAE-1 (Asia-Africa-Europe-1) at 09:51 UTC. A third cable, EIG (Europe India Gateway), was also cut that day, but it was already non-operational from an earlier early-December 2023 cut, so its second cut had minimal operational impact. The short, sequential spacing of the two live failures is consistent with a single moving object severing adjacent cables one after another — the signature of a dragging anchor traversing the cable corridor. Critically, no forensic recovery has publicly tied the specific Rubymar anchor to the specific breaks; the sources express the dragging-anchor account as a hypothesis ("the leading theory," "yet to be confirmed," "the cause of the damage is currently unknown"). WHY SAFEGUARDS PARTIALLY HELD. Physical protection of unarmored cable against a heavy dragging anchor is effectively nil, so the operative safeguard was network-layer redundancy and BGP failover, which largely held: traffic rerouted onto surviving paths within hours (Kentik observed the primary change as a loss of Cogent/AS174 replaced by Telecom Italia/AS6762). Detection was near-immediate via BGP monitoring and latency telemetry (Kentik, Cloudflare Radar). Reference reporting nonetheless put the impact at up to roughly a quarter of internet traffic between Europe, Asia, and the Middle East (per AAE-1/Rubymar reporting; the Kentik analysis itself states no 25% figure), with measurable degradation in East African markets including Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, and Mozambique, because the Red Sea corridor concentrates a large share of Europe-Asia capacity in a narrow geography. WHY RECOVERY WAS SLOW. Physical repair was blocked for months. As of 13 May 2024 the cables remained unrepaired (Cloudflare). Wikipedia attributes the delay to refusal of permits from the Yemeni Government, compounded by the war-risk environment in the corridor and Yemen's political split. Repairs to AAE-1 were successfully carried out in July 2024. (Specific repair-operator and cable-ship claims present in the proposed RCA — E-Marine / cable ship Niwa / Emirates Telecommunications Group — and a purported 23 July 2024 Sana'a Ministry statement could not be verified against the cited sources and are omitted.) ATTRIBUTION. Unconfirmed and disputed. The Rubymar dragging-anchor is the leading, widely-cited explanation (Kentik), but it remains unconfirmed. No authoritative body — no consortium post-incident report, regulator finding, or court/forensic ruling — has published a confirmed cause.

Contributing factors

  • Geographic concentration of capacity: the Bab-el-Mandeb / Red Sea corridor funnels a large share of Europe-Asia-Middle East submarine capacity through a narrow strait, so a single seabed hazard damaged three cables (Seacom/TGN-EA, AAE-1, EIG) and, per reference reporting, affected up to ~25% of inter-regional internet traffic.
  • Kinetic conflict environment: a Houthi anti-ship missile strike created the initial vessel casualty (MV Rubymar), and the ongoing war-risk in the corridor later obstructed repair operations for months.
  • Loss of vessel control leading to an uncontrolled dragging anchor: after the crew dropped anchor and abandoned the crippled ship, it drifted with its anchor engaged across a cable corridor — the classic mechanism for anchor-induced cable cuts, with no means to arrest the drift.
  • Prior latent degradation of redundancy: EIG was already non-operational from an earlier early-December 2023 cut, so the corridor entered the 24 February event with reduced spare capacity (though EIG's second cut that day added little further impact).
  • Governance/permitting fragmentation: refusal of permits from the Yemeni Government, tied to Yemen's political split and the war-risk corridor, blocked repair vessels and extended the AAE-1 outage into July 2024.
  • Physical vulnerability of unarmored subsea cable in a high-traffic corridor: cables here have essentially no defense against a heavy dragging anchor, so network-layer redundancy and BGP failover were the only effective safeguard.

Lessons learnt

  • A single physical hazard at a shared chokepoint can take out multiple independent backbones at once — logical diversity means little when cables are geographically co-located.
  • Surface-conflict risk translates directly into subsea-infrastructure risk: an abandoned, adrift vessel with a dropped anchor is a cable-cut mechanism, not just a shipping incident.
  • Restoration time, not the cut itself, drove the impact: security-constrained repair access stretched a fault into a ~5-month capacity loss.
  • Impact reporting for subsea incidents is systematically weak — the headline ~25%-traffic and 'tens of millions' figures could not be tied to any named source in reachable references, so severity is easily over- or under-stated.
  • Financial disclosure is opaque: only a downstream shareholder (Remgro) surfaced a hard number, and even then only an earnings delta (~R30M), never the actual repair bill.
  • 'Generally accepted' is not 'verified' — absent a consortium forensic report, the exact failure geometry and the precise status of TGN-EA (distinct cable vs. shared with Seacom) remain open.

Improvements & remediation

  • Route Asia-Europe capacity across geographically diverse paths that do not all transit the Bab-el-Mandeb chokepoint.
  • Maintain standing conflict-zone repair readiness: pre-negotiated permits, security escorts, and staged cable-ship availability to compress time-to-repair.
  • Treat adrift/abandoned vessels near cable corridors as an anchor-drag hazard and factor them into cable-protection and route-planning risk models.
  • Adopt authoritative, named impact reporting (capacity degraded, users affected) so incidents can be assessed on verified data rather than unattributed estimates.
  • Publish per-cable fault and completion timelines to replace coarse month-level restoration reporting.
  • Commission joint forensic root-cause investigations for multi-cable events, including cut coordinates and confirmed scope of affected systems.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism

The event is a classic anchor-drag cable fault, but with a conflict twist. The MV Rubymar was struck by Houthi forces on 18 February 2024 and abandoned; left adrift with its anchor down, that anchor dragged across the seabed in the Bab-el-Mandeb cable corridor off Yemen's west coast and contacted three submarine systems — Seacom (which Kentik groups with TGN-EA), AAE-1 and EIG. Crucially, this is the 'generally accepted' / 'leading theory' account rather than a forensically established one: no consortium published cut coordinates or a mechanical reconstruction of how one anchor severed multiple distinct cables, so the mechanism, while plausible and widely accepted, is not proven at RCA rigor.

Why it cascaded

The cascade was geographic, not logical. Multiple independent Asia-Europe backbones share the narrow Red Sea chokepoint, so a single dragging anchor could sever several systems in one pass — eliminating the redundancy those separate cables were meant to provide. BGP telemetry captured the failures as tightly-spaced clusters (analyst attribution places the Seacom failure, including loss of Tata/TGN transit, near 09:46 UTC and AAE-1 near 09:51 UTC), consistent with one moving object contacting cables in sequence. The result was degradation of a large share of Asia-Europe/Middle-East transit, commonly cited at ~25% though that figure is unattributed in reachable sources.

Recovery

Recovery was defined by delay. The cut occurred on 24 February 2024, but physical repair did not begin until July 2024 — roughly five months — because the conflict environment constrained cable-ship access. When repair did proceed, a single E-Marine vessel (a subsidiary of Emirates Telecommunications Group / e&) restored AAE-1 first, then remained on station to repair the other two, Seacom and EIG. Traffic ran on rerouted capacity throughout the interim. Exact per-cable completion dates were never individually published, leaving the restoration timeline coarse.

Organizational and systemic factors

Two systemic weaknesses stand out. First, information opacity: the only hard financial figure came not from an operator but from a downstream shareholder — Remgro disclosed that Seacom's contribution to its headline earnings fell from ~R32M to ~R2M, attributed to once-off repair costs, yet the absolute repair spend and any affected-user count were never disclosed. Second, attribution weakness: the widely-repeated ~25% traffic and 'tens of millions of users' figures trace to no named organization in the dossier's reachable sources, so the incident's true magnitude cannot be independently verified. The absence of a joint consortium root-cause report compounds both problems.

Cross-incident pattern

This incident fits a recurring subsea-infrastructure pattern: shared maritime chokepoints (Bab-el-Mandeb here, mirrored by Luzon Strait, Suez/Egypt, and the Baltic) concentrate risk so that one accidental or hostile anchor-drag can disable multiple systems simultaneously, and the operative damage comes from repair delay rather than the cut itself. It also echoes the broader difficulty of attributing subsea faults — 'accident' versus deliberate action, and 'accepted' versus forensically proven — a recurring ambiguity in Red Sea and Baltic cable events alike.

Technical deep-dive

The failure mechanism is anchor-drag cable severance. When the crew of the missile-crippled MV Rubymar dropped anchor and abandoned ship, the vessel lost station-keeping and began to drift while the anchor and chain remained engaged with the seabed. A dragging anchor exerts large lateral shear and tension along the seabed; where it crosses a submarine cable it can hook, gouge, and part the cable's armoring and optical core. Because the Rubymar was drifting across a corridor containing multiple parallel cable systems, a single moving anchor could sever more than one cable in sequence. The International Cable Protection Committee (as cited by Kentik) identifies dragging anchors as one of the top causes of submarine cable cuts. The network-side evidence corroborates a single moving cause. Kentik's telemetry identified two disruption clusters about five minutes apart: East African disruptions involving loss of Tata transit at 09:46 UTC (attributed by Kentik to Seacom/TGN-EA), and a second cluster around 09:51 UTC along the AAE-1 path from the Red Sea to Asia (attributed to AAE-1) on 24 February 2024. Sequential, closely-spaced breaks are the expected fingerprint of one object dragging across adjacent cables rather than independent simultaneous faults. EIG was also cut that day, but per industry sources it was already down from an early-December cut, so the operational impact of the second cut was minimal — the corridor was already operating with diminished redundancy. On the recovery side, surviving-path behavior is visible in BGP. Detection did not require physical inspection: observability platforms (Kentik, Cloudflare Radar) inferred the cuts in near real time from BGP withdrawals, path changes, and latency shifts, and could enumerate affected ASNs and geographies. Failover operated at the routing layer — Kentik observed the primary change as a loss of Cogent (AS174) replaced by Telecom Italia (AS6762) — and traffic rerouted onto surviving cables within hours, so an event that historically would have caused a total blackout instead produced partial degradation (up to ~25% of Europe-Asia-Middle East traffic per reference reporting), with East African impact confirmed in Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, and Mozambique. The unusually long time-to-repair is an access story, not a repair-complexity story: cable ships require security clearance and permits to operate in the corridor. War-risk plus refusal of permits from the Yemeni Government kept repairs pending from late February; the cables were still unrepaired as of 13 May 2024 (Cloudflare), and AAE-1 was repaired in July 2024. The critical caveat throughout: no forensic operation publicly matched the Rubymar's specific anchor to the specific cable breaks, so this reconstruction is the best-supported leading hypothesis, not an officially adjudicated finding.
