      Is “Agentic AI” a Fraud?
      14 claims tested against the evidence, from token prediction to a software factory

      A widely shared video argues that agents are ordinary scripts wrapped in a next-token predictor and sold to rescue an industry that hit a wall. Each of its fourteen claims is graded here against the record: the mechanism, the measured reliability, the money, the name, and what an operations engineer should do when asked to put an agent on the BMS.

      The critique is right about the mechanism and wrong about the absolutes. A language model does predict tokens, and an agent does act only through code someone wrote: the model proposes a structured call, a harness executes it and feeds the result back. But the model is not only a lookup of likely strings, the measured record of agents is real and improving, and the money is real too. What the record does show is that reliability compounds badly over many steps, so an agent is safe where deterministic checks decide which of its guesses reach the world, and never on a plant's control loop without an interlock.

      The 14 claims tested

      A YouTube video from late August 2026 argues that “agentic AI” is ordinary programming sold under a marketing label, and that the sellers know it. This article splits the video into 14 claims and checks each one against published sources. Technical claims are checked against documentation, research and benchmarks; money claims against filings and earnings; naming claims against definitions.

      1.4 Claim types and the evidence each needs

      The claims fall into three groups, each tested with a different kind of evidence.

      • Mechanism (E1 to E7, E10, E11): what a language model and an agent do, step by step. Evidence: vendor documentation, interpretability research, benchmarks. Sections 2 to 6.
      • Economics (E8, E9, E12, E13): who is spending, who is leaving, why companies would favour one story. Evidence: filings, earnings releases, press reports. Section 7.
      • Naming (E6, E7, E14): whether “AI” and “agent” are accurate names for the system. Evidence: definitions and usage, not measurement. Section 8.

      Sections 2–6 cover mechanism, measured reliability and failure modes; section 7 the money; section 8 the name. Sections 9 and 10 give design rules and an operations checklist for a data-center team. Article 29 explains the underlying model in detail, including sampling and post-training; this article cites it instead of repeating it.

      Terminology follows common usage. An agent here means a model running in a loop with tools. The wider category, an agentic system, includes the surrounding code. The word “fraud” appears only inside quotations: it is a legal claim, and section 7 lists the evidence that exists for it.

      How a language model produces text and calls tools

      The video’s technical core is correct. A language model produces one token at a time, cannot reach the internet by itself, and an “agent” is a program that takes the model’s output, runs something and returns the result. The dispute starts at “did nothing”, tested in sections 3 and 4.

      2.1 Next-token prediction

      A forward pass ends in a probability for each token in the vocabulary. The decoder picks one, appends it to the context and runs the pass again, until a stop condition is met. Elder’s description (E1, 00:48–01:43) matches this [S1]. Article 29, section on sampling, covers the logits, softmax and choice among top candidates. Consequence for agents: the same prompt can give a different answer on the next run, and nothing inside the model guarantees an output is correct, only that it is probable.

      Weights are fixed at inference; the model does not learn from the conversation. Within a session it “remembers” only the text in its context window; across sessions, only text another program stored and pasted back. Elder’s “no memory” (E3) is accurate for the weights and incomplete for the system, because agents add memory outside the model: files, databases, retrieved documents [S20].

      2.2 The model never executes anything

      Anthropic’s tool-use documentation: Claude “returns a structured call that your application executes (client tools) or that Anthropic executes (server tools)”, signalled with stop_reason: "tool_use"; “your code executes the operation and sends back a tool_result” [S15]. OpenAI’s function-calling guide lists five steps: send a request with tools, receive a tool call, “execute code on the application side with input from the tool call”, send a second request with the tool output, receive a final answer or further calls [S16]. The first model trained to choose which API to call, when, and with what arguments was described in 2023 [S17]. Two vendors and one research paper describe the same tool calling mechanism Elder describes. E4 is right: a bare model cannot book a table; it emits text and the harness does the booking.

      For an operations team: every capability of an agent is a capability of the code that executes its tool calls. Its permissions, network reach and blast radius are those of the credentials its executor holds. Section 9 builds on this; the figure comparing the video’s account with the documented one is in section 4.

      2.3 No calculator, no database lookup, no weight updates

      Three further statements are correct for a bare model:

      • No calculator inside. Arithmetic runs on learned circuits, not an arithmetic unit; section 3 shows those circuits and why they differ from a calculator [S4].
      • No database lookup at run time. Facts live in the weights, compressed and approximate, so a wrong fact comes out as fluently as a right one (Article 29).
      • Nothing learned during the conversation. A correction in turn five is available in turn six only because the text of turn five is re-sent.

      A component that can be fluent and wrong, cannot look anything up without an executor, and cannot update itself needs checks outside the component. That is the design rule at the end of section 3.

      Operator takeaway

      Read an agent’s tool list like a contractor’s access request. The model proposes; the tools act. If a tool can write to the BMS, so can the agent, whatever the system prompt says. Put permissions in the executor, where code enforces them, not in the prompt.

      2.4 Grades for this section

      E1 (next-token prediction): supported [S15] [S16]. E4 (a bare model cannot act on the internet): supported [S15] [S16]. E5, as a generic claim that a model emits a call and ordinary code runs it: supported; the demo he uses to illustrate it is not what he says it is (section 4). E2 and E3 add “no reasoning” and “no maths”, which go beyond the evidence; section 3 tests them.

      Can a language model reason or do arithmetic?

      E2 and E3 say a language model has no logic, no reasoning and no understanding of numbers. Researchers have traced what happens inside models, so this is testable. Result: the absolute version is contradicted by direct evidence of multi-step computation. A weaker version is well supported: a model cannot be trusted to reason reliably, to report what it did, or to generalise like a person. The weaker version gives the design rule at the end of this section.

      3.1 How a model adds 36 + 59

      Anthropic’s interpretability group published an attribution-graph study of Claude 3.5 Haiku in March 2025, using circuit tracing to follow which internal features feed which [S3] [S4]. For 36 + 59 = there are two parallel pathways. One carries a rough magnitude: “low-precision features for ‘add something near 57’” feed a “the sum is near 92” feature. The other carries the exact last digit through modular features. “These combine to give the correct sum of 95” [S4]. Some components are lookup-table features, for example “ends in 6” plus “ends in 9” giving “ends in 5” [S4].

      There is no calculator in the network, as E3 says. But it is not a guess either: it is a learned, approximate-plus-exact adder that the researchers could intervene on and change the output in the predicted way.

      The same study supports the caution. Asked how it got 95, the model “describes the standard algorithm involving carrying the 1”, which is not what the trace shows [S3]. Anthropic’s reading: the model “learns to explain math by simulating explanations written by people, but… has to learn to do math ‘in its head’” [S3]. On a hard cosine, the model sometimes produced an answer “without caring whether it is true or false”, with “no evidence at all of that calculation having occurred”; given a hint, it worked backwards to the hinted answer [S3]. This is chain-of-thought faithfulness: a model’s account of its reasoning is not evidence of what it did.

      3.2 Multi-step recall and planning ahead

      Asked for the capital of the state containing Dallas, the model activates a “Dallas” to “Texas” feature, then “Texas” plus “capital” to “Austin”. Swapping the Texas features for California features changes the answer to Sacramento [S3] [S4]. A direct Dallas-to-Austin shortcut also exists, so composition and memorisation both appear [S4]. In a rhyming couplet ending “grab it”, features for “rabbit” and “habit” are active on the newline before the second line is written; suppressing “rabbit” yields another planned rhyme, and injecting “green” makes the model re-plan the line [S3] [S4]. A one-token-at-a-time predictor can hold a plan several tokens ahead in its internal state.

      Limits, as the authors state them: the method traces a simpler “replacement model” and “simplifies the true mechanisms considerably” [S4]. These are case studies on short prompts, from the lab that built the model. They show “no reasoning whatsoever” is false for these tasks. They do not show reliable reasoning in general.

      3.3 Reasoning models

      A reasoning model is further trained with reinforcement learning on tasks whose answers a program can check, called RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards). It learns to write long chains of intermediate text and spends more computation at answer time (test-time compute). Article 29 covers the method. DeepSeek-R1 reports “the emergent development of advanced reasoning patterns, such as self-reflection, verification, and dynamic strategy adaptation” from pure RL without human-labelled reasoning traces [S8]. R1 scores 79.8% on AIME 2024 pass@1 (the paper’s summary figure, re-read 2026-10-25); the rise from 15.6% to 71.0% for R1-Zero during RL is reported and was not found in the extracted text [S8]. pass@1 is the fraction of problems solved on the first sample.

      3.5 Definition used, and grade for E2 and E3

      Definition used here, because it can be measured: reasoning is producing correct answers to problems that require composing several steps that are not memorised as a whole, measured on held-out or perturbed problems. The record under it: circuit studies show composition in short cases [S3] [S4]; verifiable-reward training raises scores on checkable tasks [S8]; perturbation tests expose brittleness [S7]; the hardest generalisation benchmark still shows a large human–machine gap at the cost point measured [S12].

      E2 and E3: partly supported. Computation happens, so “no logic” fails. Reliability, self-knowledge and generalisation are limited, so “you cannot trust it blindly” holds. A model can be right for reasons it cannot describe, and wrong with a confident explanation.

      Design rule

      Treat every model output as a proposal. Put something deterministic between the proposal and the plant: a schema check, a test run, a range limit, a second data source, a person. “I ran the tests and they pass” is a claim; the harness must run the tests and read the exit code.

      What an agent is: model, harness, tools

      An agent is a loop. A program shows a language model the goal and the history, the model proposes the next action, ordinary code checks and executes it, the result is added to the history, and the cycle repeats until a stop condition. The model does the one thing a conventional program cannot do cheaply: turn untidy input (free text, logs, a screenshot, an error message) into a plausible next step without a rule written for that case. Everything else is conventional software.

      4.2 The video’s restaurant example

      Elder’s example of “the LLM did nothing” is OpenAI’s restaurant-booking demo, which he describes as probably a Python program calling probably the OpenTable API (E5, 03:50–05:11) [S1]. The demo came from Operator, a research preview announced on 2025-01-23, “powered by a Computer-Using Agent model, or CUA”, trained “to interact with the front-end of websites, meaning it doesn’t need to use developer-facing APIs”. The model “can use buttons, navigate menus, and fill out forms on a web page much like a human would”, and was “trained to ask for user confirmation before finalizing tasks with external side effects” [S58]. So the booking was not an API script: a vision model read screenshots of a web page and chose clicks (a computer-using agent). OpenTable as a named launch partner could not be confirmed; launch coverage lists DoorDash, eBay, Instacart, Priceline, StubHub and Uber [S58].

      The example chosen to show the model contributes nothing is one where the model did the part a script cannot do: read an arbitrary page and decide what to do on it. The generic mechanism in his account (section 2) is correct; this example does not illustrate it.

      4.4 Workflow or agent: six levels of autonomy

      Anthropic: “Workflows are systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths. Agents… are systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage” [S14]. In a workflow the model fills in steps inside a fixed skeleton. LangChain (July 2026) describes six levels: hand-written logic, a single LLM call, a chain, a router, a state machine, an autonomous agent, graded by three questions: who decides the output of each step, who decides which step comes next, and who decides what steps are available [S20].

      Anthropic’s advice to builders agrees with the critic: look for “the simplest solution possible… This might mean not building agentic systems at all”, because agents bring “higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors”; it recommends extensive testing “in sandboxed environments, along with the appropriate guardrails” [S14]. An orchestrator that calls other models is another point on the same scale.

      For the BMS team

      Ask which of the three decisions the model makes: the output, the next step, the set of available steps. If only the output, it is a drafting tool inside a workflow, easier to test and bound. Each further decision given to the model needs its own deterministic check.

      Two of the author’s counter-claims (C1–C6, graded in section 11) are tested here. C2, that the model acts as a semantic router or dynamic planner over unstructured intent: supported [S14] [S20] [S58]. C3, that observe, act and reflect (run tests, read the error, fix) is a genuine pattern: supported as a pattern, only partly as a new one, since ReAct dates from 2022 [S18] and closed-loop control is far older.

      Measured reliability of agents

      Agents are scored on pass/fail tasks, mostly in software. Scores are high and rising. The same data show success falling sharply as the required reliability rises, and several well-known benchmarks losing credibility through tuning and contamination. Capability has grown fast; reliability more slowly, and reliability is what limits operational use.

      5.1 Coding benchmarks: SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench

      SWE-bench Verified asks an agent to resolve real GitHub issues in open-source projects, judged by the project’s tests. The official leaderboard data, read on 2026-10-16, put the top entry at 79.2% (two systems, each using Claude 4.5 Opus), followed by 78.8% and 77.4%; the Verified board has no entries after December 2025 [S22]. In February 2026 OpenAI announced that it no longer evaluates on Verified, citing flawed tests and contamination, and recommended SWE-bench Pro [S23]. OpenAI audited a 27.6% subset of the dataset that models often failed and found that “59.4% of the 138 problems contained material issues in test design and/or problem description”, and that the open repositories make contamination hard to avoid: “We first encountered signs of contamination in our own models” [S23] (page re-read 2026-10-25). The fall from about 70% on Verified to about 23% on Pro is not on OpenAI’s page; it comes from a news report and is reported, unverified [S24].

      A score near 80% on a retired benchmark does not mean “agents fix 80% of real issues”. It means agents with good harnesses pass most of a test set that is partly flawed and may have leaked into training data.

      Terminal-Bench 2.0 uses 89 tasks in containers, each with a human-written oracle solution. Top entries on the mirrored leaderboard are 84.7% (±2.1), 84.5% (±2.6), 83.1% and 82.2% [S25]. The same model scores differently under different harnesses: the “plumbing” the video dismisses changes outcomes [S25].

      5.3 Developer productivity trials (METR, 2025 and 2026)

      METR’s randomised trial from early 2025: experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI tools (confidence interval +2% to +39%) while believing they were faster [S28] [S29]. A follow-up published 2026-02-24 estimates the change in time at −18% for returning developers (CI −38% to +9%) and −4% for new ones (CI −15% to +9%). METR calls the data “an unreliable signal” because developers declined to work without AI, pay fell from $150 to $50 per hour, and time tracking was unreliable. It now believes late-2025 tools “likely” sped developers up, but the size is unknown [S29].       5.4 Error compounding over many steps

      A multi-step task succeeds only if every step does. If each step succeeds with probability p and the steps are independent, a run of n steps succeeds with p^n:

      P(run) = pn
0.9520 = 0.358        (20 steps at 95% each)
0.99100 = 0.366       (100 steps at 99% each)

      Anthropic names compounding errors as a cost of agents [S14]. A deterministic verifier changes the arithmetic. Suppose a check catches a bad step with probability d and the step is retried up to k times. With p = 0.95, d = 0.9 and k = 2, the chance of an undetected-bad step falls and a step succeeds with probability 0.95 × 1.04706 = 0.9947, so a 20-step run succeeds with 0.9947^20 = 0.899 (derived; the full model is in the calculator below). The extra attempts raise the cost per run from $1.76 to $1.84, about 5% (1.843/1.760 = 1.047), under the calculator’s default prices and token counts.

      Run cost and reliability with compounding error

      This estimator shows why a 95%-per-step agent fails most 20-step runs and what a verifier with retries changes. Each attempt is good with probability p; a bad attempt is caught with probability d and retried up to k times. It computes q = (1 − p) · d, E = (1 − q^(k+1)) / (1 − q), Ps = p · E and P_run = Ps^n, then cost_run = n · E · cost_att and cost_per_success = cost_run / P_run. Steps are assumed independent, which is optimistic.

      Caveats (the calculator prints them): independence is optimistic because real errors correlate, and a model-based verifier may share the generator’s blind spots. The METR 50-versus-80 gap is the measured version of this curve [S27].

      Operator takeaway

      20 steps at 95% per step is a one-in-three system. The cheapest improvement is a deterministic check at each step; a bigger model is the expensive one. Ask any vendor for the success rate at your required run length, not for a benchmark peak.

      Agent failure modes and mitigations

      A script fails the same way each time and reports where. An agent can fail differently on each run, be steered by text arriving through a tool, report a step it did not take, and spend a large budget unnoticed. Each failure mode below is documented and has a standard mitigation; in each, the check does not depend on the model agreeing that a failure occurred.

      6.1 Prompt injection through data

      A language model has no reliable way to distinguish the instructions it was given from instructions written inside the content it reads. OWASP’s entry LLM01:2025 describes prompt injection in its indirect form: “Indirect prompt injections occur when an LLM accepts input from external sources, such as websites or files.” Listed impacts include “Executing arbitrary commands in connected systems” and “Manipulating critical decision-making processes” [S31]. Relevant inputs in operations: alarm text, ticket bodies, vendor PDFs, e-mail, CMMS free-text fields. An agent that reads a ticket and has a tool that acts on it gives whoever can write a ticket a path to whatever the tool can do.

      Section 3 described the carry-the-1 explanation that did not match the circuit [S3]. In agent work it appears as a status line: “tests pass”, “configuration applied”, “alarm cleared”. That line is a claim. The harness runs the test or reads the sensor; the model’s text is commentary.

      6.5 Retry loops and quadratic token cost

      An agent re-reads its history on every step, so input tokens grow with the square of the step count. With a 20,000-token starting context and 3,000 tokens added per step, step 30 has consumed 1,905,000 cumulative input tokens: 30 × 20,000 + 3,000 × 30 × 29 / 2 = 600,000 + 1,305,000 (derived). At the list prices used in this article’s calculators ($2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output, from Article 29) that is $3.81 of input plus $0.24 of output, or $4.05. A stuck retry loop reaches that cost with no one typing. The budget planner in section 9 sets limits before a run starts.

      Common mitigation: checks outside the model

      Mitigations: argument schemas and allow-lists (tool misuse), scoped credentials (privilege), a sandbox (code execution), treating all fetched text as untrusted data (injection), a harness-run verifier (false reports), step and cost limits (loops), and a human approval for anything irreversible. A hard gate is a check whose failure cannot be overridden by the agent. The more of these exist in code, the less the system depends on the model behaving.

      When to use an agent, and how to build one

      The engineering question is where a probabilistic component belongs in an otherwise deterministic system. The evidence gives one answer: let the model do what only a model can do (read untidy input, choose among options, draft) and put every consequence behind a check that does not rely on the model.       9.2 Reference architecture: probabilistic planner, deterministic executor

      Six parts in a line: A probabilistic planner (the LLM) proposes an action. A typed tool interface validates it against a schema and an allow-list. Deterministic executors (scripts, APIs, compilers) carry it out. Verifiers (tests, linters, type checkers, simulators, policy checks) judge the result. A human gate stands in front of irreversible actions. An audit log records everything. Budgets on steps, tokens, time and cost are enforced outside the model. The model never holds credentials directly and never writes to safety-rated systems [S14] [S31] [S32] [S33]. Anthropic on the verifier: the agent should get “ground truth from the environment at each step” [S14].

      The seam between the halves: the model sees and produces text; the interface turns that text into a typed call or rejects it with a reason that goes back to the model; the executor and verifier produce facts. The model’s explanations of what it did are kept out of the chain of evidence.

      9.4 Run budgets: cost, wall clock, context window

      Three limits bind an agent run: money, wall-clock time and the context window. Because history is re-read each step, cost grows with the square of the step count. With c0 the starting context, g the tokens added per step and S the number of steps:

      input tokens to step S = S × c0 + g × S(S − 1) / 2
Cost(S) = price_in × [S × c0 + g × S(S − 1)/2] / 106 + price_out × S × o / 106
t_step  = TTFT + o × TPOT + t_tool
max_steps = min(S_cost, S_time, S_context)

      With defaults of 20,000 starting tokens, 3,000 tokens added per step, 800 output tokens per step, a $5 cap, a 600 s wall clock and 5 s per tool call, at $2 and $10 per million tokens: Cost(10) = $0.75, Cost(20) = $2.10, Cost(30) = $4.05, Cost(40) = $6.60. The cost cap allows 34 steps. One step takes 0.5 + 800 × 0.02 + 5 = 21.5 s (the latency constants are assumptions from Article 29), so the wall clock allows floor(600 / 21.5) = 27 steps. The context limit, compaction at 80% of a 200,000-token window, allows floor((160,000 − 20,000) / 3,000) + 1 = 47. The binding limit is the wall clock, at 27 steps, and a per-step timeout near 65 s (three times the step time) is suggested. Caching changes the slope of the cost curve, not its shape.

      Design rules

      Use the simplest system that works. Put validation, execution, verification and limits in code. Give every irreversible action a human gate. Add verifiers that fail for different reasons. When an agent’s path for a class of task becomes stable, replace it with a script: a cheap, testable routine.

      Checklist: connecting an agent to a BMS

      When asked to connect an agent to the building management system, specify what the agent may read, what it may write, and what stands between its suggestion and the plant. Government guidance on AI in operational technology (OT) matches the architecture in section 9; the checklist below applies it. Rule: read freely, propose in writing, and let only the existing control system, with its interlocks and a person, act.

      10.1 Guidance used

      • CISA, ASD’s ACSC, NSA’s AI Security Center, the FBI and five partner agencies, Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology (published 2025-12-03; PDF revision January 2026). Four principles: understand AI; consider AI use in the OT domain; establish AI governance and assurance frameworks; embed oversight and failsafe practices. It advises to “limit active control of OT infrastructure by AI without a human in the loop to account for safety concerns and latency limitations” and to “regularly update and validate AI models” [S33]. The phrase is human in the loop.
      • NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, released 2023-01-26 [S34]. Its four functions are commonly given as Govern, Map, Measure and Manage; the document was not re-read for this article.
      • ISO/IEC 42001:2023, a management-system standard for AI (ISO/IEC 42001); its scope was not re-read for this article [S35].
      • IEC 62443 for security zones and conduits in industrial systems and IEC 61511 for safety instrumented systems (IEC 62443, SIS); cited as background, not read for this article [S36]. NIST’s framework is covered in the glossary under NIST AI RMF.
      • OWASP LLM01 and the agentic Top 10 for injection and tool risks [S31] [S32].

      10.2 Ten-point checklist

      1. Read-only first. The agent reads BMS, EPMS and DCIM trends, alarms and CMMS history. It writes only to a ticket, a draft MOP or a chat message.
      2. No autonomous write to OT, BMS or EPMS. A setpoint, mode or breaker change goes through the existing control system with its interlocks, a human approval and a change record [S33]. Safety functions (SIS, fire, EPO, leak-detection trips) stay out of scope entirely [S36].
      3. One-way data path. The agent sits on the IT side of the DMZ. OT data flows out through a historian replica, and no inbound conduit leads from the agent’s zone to the control zone [S33] [S36].
      4. Typed, allow-listed tools with range checks. A supply-air setpoint limit of, say, 18 to 27 °C is enforced in code, not in the prompt. (The numbers are an illustration, not a recommendation.)
      5. A deterministic verifier per action. A simulator, a rule check or a second data source. For example, confirm a CRAH fault from two independent sensors before raising a priority-one alarm.
      6. Every document, ticket and alarm text is untrusted input. It can carry instructions the agent should not follow [S31] [S32].
      7. Budgets and a kill switch. Limits on steps, cost and wall-clock time, and a manual disable that does not depend on the agent.
      8. Logging and replay. Keep prompts, tool calls and results for incident review, which supports the measure and manage functions in NIST’s framework [S34].
      9. Evaluate before rollout. Use a fixed set of historical incidents with a known correct triage; track precision and recall for each release, and rerun the set when the model or prompt changes.
      10. Codify what repeats. When the agent’s procedure for an alarm class is stable, turn it into a deterministic rule and retire the agent for that class.

      Permitted data flow: Trends and alarms move one way from the control zone to a historian replica in the DMZ, and from there to the agent. The agent can draft a work order, and it can send a proposed change with its evidence to an operator. An approved change passes through a change gateway that checks range and interlock conditions before reaching the BMS. No path leads from the agent to the BMS, the PLC or the safety system. The safety system trips equipment independently.

      “Put an agent on the BMS” becomes “put an agent next to the BMS, with read-only access, a draft-only output, and a person between its suggestion and the setpoint.” Anything beyond that needs its own risk assessment against the guidance [S33].

      Findings

      The video is right about the mechanism and wrong about the absolutes. The system it criticises is a language model inside a loop of ordinary code, as it says; the model does real work inside that loop, which it denies; and the label is loose in ways that analysts have documented. The evidence read does not show that the economics are anything worse than hype.

      11.1 Correct claims in the video

      A model predicts one token at a time and cannot act on the world by itself; vendors document exactly this [S15] [S16]. Its explanations of its own steps can be false [S3], its accuracy can drop sharply under small perturbations [S7], it generalises worse than people by the account of a scaling pioneer [S54], and its reliability at a high success rate is far below its reliability at a low one [S27]. The executor half of an agent is decades-old engineering. Marketing has inflated the word “agent”, and an analyst firm has a name for it [S39].       11.4 Recommendations and open questions

      Use the model where input is untidy and the answer can be checked; keep it away from anything that moves equipment; measure success at the run length you need; and keep the exit cost low while the evidence settles. Open questions remain: the true size of the productivity effect, how well verification holds up when the checker shares the generator’s blind spots, and what the 2026 benchmark scores really are. A later edition will revisit them.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is an AI agent, in engineering terms?

      An agent is a language model running in a loop with tools. The model proposes the next action, ordinary code validates and executes it, and the result goes back into the model's context until a stop condition is met. Anthropic defines agents as typically just LLMs using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop. The model handles untidy input and chooses steps; deterministic code handles execution, permissions, budgets and logging. The model never runs anything itself. Every capability of an agent is a capability of the code that executes its tool calls [S14][S15][S16].

      Is agentic AI a fraud, as one popular video claims?

      The video's mechanism description is largely correct: a model predicts one token at a time and cannot act without code that executes its calls [S15][S16]. Its absolutes do not hold. Interpretability work found multi-step computation inside a model [S3][S4], and the restaurant demo it cites used a vision-based browser agent, not an API script [S58]. The securities-fraud remark is hedged by the speaker himself, and no regulator action alleging it was found in a limited search [S1]. The marketing critique has substance: analysts name it agent washing [S39].

      Can a language model reason or do maths?

      Partly. Anthropic's attribution-graph study of Claude 3.5 Haiku found two parallel pathways when adding 36 and 59, one for rough magnitude and one for the exact last digit [S3][S4]. The same study found the model's account of carrying the 1 did not match what it did [S3]. Apple's GSM-Symbolic found that adding one clause can cut accuracy by up to 65% [S7]. The practical rule is to treat output as a proposal and check it with something deterministic, such as a test, a schema or a second data source.

      How reliable are agents on long tasks?

      Reliability falls as the required success rate rises. METR measures task length in human time. Its early Claude Mythos Preview result was a 50% time horizon of 1,045 minutes (about 17.4 hours) but an 80% horizon of only 186 minutes (about 3.1 hours), a ratio of 5.6 [S27]. Independent steps compound: 0.95 per step over 20 steps gives 0.95 to the power 20, or 0.358 (derived). A deterministic verifier with retries can lift that run to about 0.90 for roughly 5% more tokens in the article's model.

      Is the AI industry out of private money and forced to list?

      The evidence is mixed. An IPO motive exists: Anthropic confidentially submitted a draft S-1 on 2026-06-01 [S49], and OpenAI said an IPO in 2026 would be ill-advised [S52]. But the two largest private rounds on record closed in the same half-year: OpenAI raised $122B at $852B on 2026-03-31 [S51] and Anthropic raised $65B at $965B on 2026-05-28 [S48]. Big-four capex guidance for 2026 sums to roughly $0.70 to $0.74 trillion (derived) [S40][S45]. The video's figure of $3 trillion is unverified.

      Can an agent be safely connected to a building management system?

      Safely means read-only access plus draft outputs, with a person and the existing control system between a suggestion and a setpoint. CISA, ASD's ACSC and partner agencies advise to limit active control of OT infrastructure by AI without a human in the loop [S33]. The article's ten-point checklist adds a one-way data path through a historian replica, typed and range-checked tools, untrusted-input handling per OWASP [S31][S32], budgets with a kill switch, and keeping safety functions out of scope entirely.
