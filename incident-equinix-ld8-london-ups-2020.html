Equinix LD8 Docklands UPS Failure: ~17.5-Hour Power Outage Disrupts LINX and ~150 Members

Equinix 2020-08-18 17h 30m core impact Power

On 18 August 2020 a faulty UPS system at Equinix's LD8 Docklands data centre — home to the London Internet Exchange (LINX) — cut power across levels 1-4 for roughly 17.5 hours to full resolution. Onset was around 04:23-04:28 BST (Giganet observed feed loss at 04:23; BT's interruption began 04:28); Equinix declared all services resolved at 21:50 BST. Per The Register, the failure of an output static switch on a Galaxy UPS serving levels 1-4 tripped the facility fire alarm (no actual fire), causing a safety power-down of those floors that took out even the 'diverse' A and B feeds customers were contracted for. Customer evidence (Giganet) confirms both A and B feeds to one rack died simultaneously at ~04:23. Around 150 LINX members were affected, with named downstream ISPs — including BT, Sky and Virgin Media — knocked offline. Recovery was staggered and uneven: some services (e.g. BT) were back by ~08:03 while others waited into the evening, compounded because the site's access-control system also went dark, leaving customers queuing outside unable to badge in. Equinix's only official root-cause wording was 'a faulty UPS system'; it announced a review into power-supply resiliency but never published a formal RCA.

Root cause

Trigger. In the early hours of 18 August 2020 (customer-observed onset ~04:24 BST; Equinix's stated onset 04:40), the output static switch of a Schneider 'Galaxy' UPS system serving levels 1 through 4 of building 8/9 at Equinix's LD8 (Docklands, former Telecity) IBX failed. Equinix's own diagnosis, relayed via a customer IBX update quoted by The Register, named the root cause as 'a faulty UPS (uninterrupted power supply) system,' and stated that 'the fire alarm was triggered by the failure of output static switch from Galaxy UPS system supporting levels 1, 2, 3, 4' in building 8/9. The output static switch is the solid-state transfer element on the UPS output that selects between the inverter path and the bypass/mains path; its failure is the point at which conditioned power to the downstream distribution serving those four levels was compromised. Mechanism and escalation. The static-switch failure triggered the site fire alarm even though there was no actual fire. In accordance with site safety procedures, all systems in the vicinity of the fault across levels 1 to 4 were then powered down and the IBX was evacuated. The escalation path was therefore: electrical fault -> fire-alarm activation -> procedural mass power-down of an entire four-level block. The blast radius was driven as much by the safety response to the alarm as by the electrical fault itself. Why redundancy did not protect customers. Customers at LD8 were provisioned with dual 'A+B diverse' power feeds, the standard arrangement intended to survive loss of one supply path. This did not hold. Customer Matthew Skipsey reported that the alarm 'triggered the loss of both our A+B diverse power feeds to our main rack since 04:24,' and LINX reported that 'Simultaneously we lost power to our own A and B power feeds and subsequently our equipment within LD8.' Because the affected racks were fed from within the levels 1-4 block that was powered down, the 'diverse' feeds shared a common upstream dependency, so the loss was common-mode rather than independent. No official Equinix statement explains WHY both diverse feeds were lost together; the common-mode conclusion is an inference from converging customer reports plus the fact that the whole levels 1-4 zone was de-energised. (Note: a further specific timing and a 'clearly unacceptable / lack of resiliency' complaint have been attributed to customer Giganet, but that could not be verified against an accessible source and is excluded here.) Failover and suppression. There is no evidence that an alternative source (generator or alternate UPS string) automatically picked up the affected racks; the safety-driven power-down removed power from the zone, and recovery required manual work, including at least one operator (M12/Giganet) reporting its LD8 core router had been 'connected to a temporary power feed.' Fire suppression was not involved because there was no fire; the fire ALARM, not suppression, was the propagation mechanism. Downstream escalation and recovery. The outage propagated into the wider internet because LD8 'hosts one of the eleven points of presence for LINX LON1 and LON2 peering LANs'; 'approximately 150 LINX members will have been directly affected,' and a small number of BT Enterprise/Global Ethernet customers 'experienced an interruption to their Ethernet services between 04:28 and 08:03.' LINX reported power being restored to the site from 11:46 and 'all of LINX devices have been restored' by 13:42. A whole-site restoration time of 21:49 has been cited elsewhere but could not be verified in the accessible sources. Caveat on authority. Equinix publicly named the cause (faulty UPS) and the failing component (Galaxy UPS output static switch) via a brief customer update quoted by The Register, but no detailed engineering post-incident report was published. The deeper questions -- why the static switch failed, why its failure raised a fire alarm, and why the A+B diverse feeds were commonly dependent -- are not answered by any authoritative Equinix root-cause document in the available evidence. Official confirmation. This pass newly established a genuine primary official source: the LINX (London Internet Exchange) incident statement, a first-party record from an operator directly affected inside LD8. I fetched it directly on 2026-08-01 and verified all three quotes verbatim. It officially confirms the outage mechanism — loss of Equinix-supplied power at ~04:30 BST and the SIMULTANEOUS loss of both A and B feeds — and relays Equinix's confirmation of whole-site power restoration by 21:49 BST. Critical honesty note: LINX does NOT use the word 'UPS' and gives no root cause, so the widely-reported UPS attribution is corroborated by the mechanism but NOT confirmed by any retrieved primary Equinix document; Equinix's customer RFO remains non-public and unreachable here (press pages 403/404, archive.org blocked, WebSearch budget exhausted). officialPostmortem is set true on the strength of the LINX first-party official statement, with the UPS-specific cause flagged as press-sourced rather than officially confirmed.

Contributing factors

  • Common-mode dependency in the 'A+B diverse' power topology: independent customers (Matthew Skipsey; LINX) reported losing BOTH nominally diverse feeds simultaneously (~04:24), indicating the A and B paths for affected racks shared an upstream dependency on the Galaxy UPS system / levels 1-4 power block rather than being truly independent. No Equinix statement confirms the coupling mechanism; it is inferred from converging reports.
  • Safety-procedure-driven mass power-down amplified the blast radius: a UPS fault escalated to a fire alarm with no actual fire, and standing safety procedures then required powering down all systems across levels 1-4 and evacuating the IBX, converting a single-component fault into a four-level outage.
  • Single-component criticality of the UPS output static switch: the failure of one static switch on the Galaxy UPS serving four levels was sufficient to both compromise conditioned power and trigger the alarm cascade, indicating limited fault isolation between that switch and the fire-detection/safety-shutdown logic.
  • Absence of automatic failover to an unaffected source for the de-energised zone: recovery for at least one customer depended on manually connecting equipment to a temporary power feed rather than an automatic transfer, indicating the safety shutdown left no live redundant path within levels 1-4.
  • Recurrence at the same facility: the former Telecity LD8 site had a prior July 2016 'brief outage' that took roughly 10 per cent of BT internet subscribers offline, suggesting a latent history of power-plant vulnerability at this location (the 2016 event's specific cause was not disclosed and is not confirmed to be UPS-related).

Correction of errors (COE)

  • Conduct a review into the resiliency of Equinix's power supplies (the single stated corrective action following the outage). Equinix · Announced / committed (no published findings)
  • Investigate and eliminate the common-mode dependency between the 'diverse' A and B feeds so a single UPS or static-switch failure cannot take down both sides. Equinix engineering · Implied by resiliency review; no public confirmation
  • Decouple the physical access-control system from the affected power domain (or provide independent backup) so customers can badge in during a power event. Equinix facilities · Not stated / unconfirmed
  • Publish a formal RCA/correction-of-errors and improve incident communications with a defined acknowledgement SLA and restoration ETAs. Equinix · Not done (no formal RCA published)

Lessons learnt

  • 'Diverse' A+B feeds are only as resilient as their least-shared common component — if both paths trace back to one UPS or output static switch, the redundancy is nominal, not real, and a single failure defeats 2N.
  • Life-safety interlocks (fire alarm / EPO) can become amplification paths in a power event; a static-switch fault that trips the alarm can force a safety power-down of whole floors, so this coupling must be understood and constrained.
  • Ancillary systems on the critical path — here, physical access-control — must survive a power failure, or recovery that depends on customer hands-on access is blocked at the front door.
  • Communication is part of resilience: an ~8-hour silence with no ETA magnifies customer harm even while engineers work the technical fix.
  • History is not a guarantee: LD8's 2016 UPS event was resolved within minutes affecting few customers; the same failure class in 2020 hit levels 1-4, ~150 members and ran until evening for many tenants — prior mild outcomes should not breed complacency.
  • Facility-level failures at internet-exchange sites have outsized blast radius, cascading to downstream ISPs and carriers (BT, Sky, Virgin Media) well beyond direct tenants.

Improvements & remediation

  • Audit every 'diverse' A/B feed pair end-to-end to confirm true physical and electrical separation with no shared UPS, output static switch, or busway single point of failure.
  • Provide genuinely independent redundant UPS paths (separate systems, separate output switching) for critical loads, verified by pull-the-plug testing.
  • Isolate or independently power life-safety interlocks so a fire-alarm/EPO trip cannot needlessly de-energise redundant power sides across whole floors.
  • Put physical access-control on protected/backup power (or an offline-capable fallback) so customers can reach their equipment during an outage.
  • Adopt a formal incident-communications standard: acknowledge within a defined window and provide rolling restoration ETAs.
  • Publish a formal RCA/COE after major incidents to enable customer trust and industry learning.
  • Institute periodic 2N-failover drills and independent resiliency audits rather than relying on design intent.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism

The trigger was a single failed UPS system, the only element Equinix ever officially named as root cause. What made a component failure catastrophic was topology and a life-safety coupling: per The Register, the failure of an output static switch on a Galaxy UPS serving levels 1-4 tripped the fire alarm, forcing a safety power-down of those floors. That took out even the A and B paths marketed as 'diverse' — Giganet's dual-fed rack lost both feeds at the same instant (~04:23 BST). Simultaneous loss of both sides is the signature of a common-mode failure: the two supposedly independent paths shared a dependency, so one fault removed both. Redundancy that shares a single point of failure provides the appearance of 2N without its protection.

Why it cascaded

Two coupling paths turned a UPS fault into a facility-wide event. First, the static-switch failure tripped the fire alarm, a life-safety interlock that forced de-energisation of levels 1-4 'per safety procedures'. Second, that power-down took the physical access-control system offline. The second cascade is what made recovery so long for many tenants: customers who travelled to LD8 to power-cycle and restore their own kit were left queuing outside, unable to badge in. The failure domain had swallowed the very system needed to fix it.

Recovery

Recovery was staggered and uneven rather than a single clean restore. Some services came back within hours — BT's interruption ended around 08:03 BST — while others waited into the evening. Equinix did not acknowledge the incident publicly until roughly eight hours after onset (around midday BST) and gave no ETA. Restoration depended heavily on hands-on customer intervention; Equinix relaxed its Covid-19 access rules (retaining temperature checks and face coverings) specifically to admit more customers to service their equipment. Equinix declared all services resolved at 21:50 BST, roughly 17.5 hours after the fault began. The dependence on customer access, combined with the offline badge system, stretched the tail of the timeline well beyond a pure operator-side repair.

Organisational and systemic factors

Beyond the electrical fault, the incident exposed organisational gaps: a ~7-8 hour communications void that customers publicly criticised (one noting the first update came around eight hours in with no ETA), and the absence of any formal published RCA — the only stated corrective action was a review 'into the resiliency of its power supplies'. Giganet, whose kit was dual-fed on contracted diverse feeds yet still lost power, concluded the facility lacked the resiliency it had paid for and called the event 'clearly unacceptable'. The lack of a public postmortem leaves the precise interlock physics only partially verified — via The Register's reporting rather than an Equinix engineering statement — and limits industry learning.

Cross-incident pattern

This event fits a recurring pattern in which nominal redundancy masks a hidden common dependency, and in which ancillary systems (cooling, controls, access) sit inside the same failure domain as the primary load. The contrast with LD8's own 2016 UPS outage — limited customers, restored within minutes per Equinix's then-UK MD — underscores that the same failure class can produce wildly different outcomes depending on blast containment. Because LD8 hosts LINX, the blast radius extended to ~150 exchange members and multiple downstream ISPs and carriers (including BT, Sky and Virgin Media), illustrating how a single facility power fault at an internet-exchange node propagates across the wider connectivity ecosystem.

Technical deep-dive

The initiating fault was in the OUTPUT STATIC SWITCH of a Schneider 'Galaxy'-class UPS system feeding levels 1-4 of building 8/9 at LD8. In a double-conversion UPS the static (bypass) switch is the fast solid-state (thyristor/SCR) transfer element on the UPS output that determines whether the load is served from the inverter or transferred to raw mains bypass. It is a single, high-current node in the power path; a fault in it can interrupt or corrupt the output regardless of whether the batteries and inverter are healthy, consistent with downstream racks losing power despite the presence of a UPS. The reported behaviour is unusual in one respect: Equinix stated the fire alarm 'was triggered by the failure of output static switch,' yet there was no fire. The sources do not state the exact detection mechanism; a static-switch failure is a plausible source of localised overheating, arcing, or smoke at the UPS/switchgear that an early-warning smoke/thermal detection system could interpret as a fire event, but this specific detector path is not documented. Once the fire alarm activated, the controlling factor became procedural: safety procedure powered down all systems in the vicinity of the fault across levels 1-4 and evacuated the IBX. This is why a single-switch fault produced a four-level outage -- the safety response, not the electrical fault alone, defined the blast radius. The critical redundancy question is why nominally independent A and B feeds failed together. Racks were dual-fed with 'diverse' A+B feeds -- the arrangement in which each rack PDU is served by two upstream distribution paths so that loss of one leaves the other live. Skipsey and LINX both report losing both feeds at essentially the same instant (~04:24). For genuinely independent paths this is improbable; the convergence indicates common-mode dependency -- the A and B paths for the affected racks ultimately drew on the same faulted UPS system or terminated within the same levels 1-4 zone that safety procedures de-energised. Powering down the whole zone removes both 'diverse' feeds at once, defeating the intended protection. No automatic transfer to a healthy source is evident; recovery for at least one operator required manually patching a core router onto a temporary power feed, implying the safety shutdown left no live redundant path within the affected block. Fire suppression is not implicated (no fire); the alarm signal, not suppression discharge, was the propagation vector. The precise electrical failure mode of the static switch, the exact detector that raised the alarm, and the topology that coupled the A/B feeds are not disclosed in any authoritative Equinix engineering report within the available evidence.
