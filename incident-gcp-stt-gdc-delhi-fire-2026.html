Google Cloud India Disruption After STT GDC Delhi Data-Center Fire

Google Cloud (impacted tenant); STT GDC / Tata Communications JV (named as the third-party facility operator only by third-party reporting; not confirmed by Google) 2026-06-05 477h 53m core impact FirePowerNetwork

On or about 5 June 2026, a fire at a third-party data-center facility in New Delhi forced an emergency power shutdown of networking equipment, isolating a non-compute Google Cloud Point of Presence (POP) in the Delhi metro and reducing available network capacity across the region. Google Cloud customers experienced elevated latency, intermittent packet loss and non-optimal routing across Delhi, Mumbai and Chennai. Google first disclosed customer impact on 9 June and worked the incident through nine status updates until resolution on 29 June, with no workaround available throughout. Recovery was gated by out-of-region capacity augmentation and, ultimately, by safety-cleared physical re-entry to the damaged site around 22-23 June. Google never named the facility, operator or ignition source; the lithium-battery/UPS attribution comes only from third-party reporting, not from Google's record.

Root cause

Authoritative network-layer cause is published in Google Cloud's Service Health report (incident 5fGQt4VbkDnr3Yp8PXPr). Per Google's own wording, a fire at a third-party data center facility required an emergency power shutdown of networking equipment, isolating a non-compute local Point of Presence (POP) in Delhi and reducing the network capacity available in the Delhi metro area. The trigger was therefore not a defect in Google's compute region (asia-south2) but a physical event at a colocation facility hosting Google network/edge infrastructure.\n\nSourcing caveat: Google does NOT name the facility, its operator, the ignition source, or any fire-suppression/backup-power detail. Attribution of the site to an STT GDC / Tata joint venture originates only from third-party press cited in the task brief; it is not stated by Google, was not independently verified here, and must not be presented as established fact.\n\nMechanism: the fire forced a deliberate, protective de-energization ('emergency power shutdown') of networking equipment, consistent with standard fire response (cutting power to protect equipment and personnel and permit suppression). De-energizing that equipment removed a non-compute local POP — an edge/peering/backbone node, not a compute cluster — from service, so Google lost a portion of the metro's Internet Edge peering capacity and backbone capacity into and out of Delhi.\n\nWhy safeguards did not fully absorb the loss: the report frames the outage as a capacity problem. Traffic that had transited the isolated Delhi POP rerouted onto remaining paths; the resulting symptom was intermittent elevated latency and possible packet loss for traffic from Delhi, Chennai, Mumbai and surrounding areas. Google's stated remediation was entirely capacity restoration, which is consistent with the surviving network having insufficient spare headroom to carry the redistributed load without degradation. (That the redistributed demand exceeded remaining capacity is a reasonable inference from Google's 'reducing available network capacity' language plus the symptom and the capacity-only fixes; it is not quoted verbatim by Google.) Google does not describe the colocation site's UPS/generator or suppression outcomes, so no claim is made about them — its stated action was to shut power off deliberately.\n\nBlast radius: because Internet Edge peering and inter-metro backbone links were affected, impact was not confined to Delhi; traffic from Delhi, Chennai, Mumbai and surrounding areas saw intermittent elevated latency and possible packet loss. Affected products were Hybrid Connectivity, Media CDN, and Virtual Private Cloud (VPC); the affected region was listed as Delhi (asia-south2). (The proposal's additional 'Global' scope tag is not supported by the fetched report and is struck.) There was no workaround.\n\nRecovery: because a physical site was damaged and a POP isolated, restoration was not automatic. Google's actions were capacity interventions: restoring Internet Edge peering capacity in Delhi, augmenting out-of-region capacity in Chennai, optimizing backbone headroom, and restoring capacity on the Delhi-Chennai and Delhi-Mumbai routes. Further restoration was gated by regaining physical access to the damaged site, which — following safety clearance — teams obtained on 2026-06-22 (16:51 PDT). The incident began 2026-06-05 00:00 US/Pacific; the first public status update appeared 2026-06-09 18:13 PDT; full capacity restoration completed 2026-06-26 (US/Pacific); and the incident was closed with a final update on 2026-06-29 16:06 PDT. Google does not state explicit detection timing, leaving an unexplained gap between the stated begin time and the first public update.

Contributing factors

  • Single-facility concentration of metro network capacity: a non-compute local POP in Delhi carried a material share of the metro's Internet Edge peering and backbone capacity, so isolating one third-party facility measurably reduced network capacity for the whole Delhi metro (per Google's report).
  • Insufficient reroute headroom / capacity redundancy: the report frames the outage as a capacity problem and its every remediation was capacity restoration, indicating the surviving network lacked enough spare headroom to absorb the lost POP without latency and packet loss.
  • Dependency on a third-party colocation facility for critical networking infrastructure: the fire, the emergency power shutdown, and the physical damage all occurred at a site Google did not own or control, placing a key part of the availability chain outside Google's direct operational management.
  • Physical-access dependency in the recovery path: full capacity restoration was gated by teams regaining access to the damaged site (obtained 2026-06-22 after safety clearance), so recovery was bounded by physical site conditions rather than remote remediation, extending the degraded period to roughly three weeks (2026-06-05 to 2026-06-26).
  • Cross-metro coupling of network paths: because the impaired capacity included inter-metro backbone links (Delhi-Chennai, Delhi-Mumbai), a localized Delhi event propagated to Chennai, Mumbai and surrounding areas rather than staying contained to Delhi.

Lessons learnt

  • A non-compute network POP can be a single point of regional degradation: losing one Delhi POP reduced capacity across three metros, showing that edge/peering redundancy matters as much as compute-region redundancy.
  • Failover headroom must be provisioned for full-node loss, not incremental load: the latency/packet-loss cascade shows surviving links were not sized to absorb the isolated POP's entire traffic share.
  • Third-party-facility incidents put recovery on someone else's clock: substantive restoration was gated by safety-cleared physical re-entry (~22-23 June), a timeline Google could not control, which stretched the outage beyond network-side fixes.
  • Out-of-region capacity is a viable but slow mitigation: augmenting Chennai peering and Delhi-Mumbai/Chennai links provided redundancy but took roughly two weeks to reach full restoration.
  • Disclosure discipline is a reputational risk: the ~4-day gap between onset and first notice, with no workaround for the full window, left tenants exposed and fueled third-party data-loss fears.
  • Provenance discipline matters in postmortems: the widely-repeated lithium/UPS ignition story is not in Google's record; conflating third-party attribution with operator-confirmed cause risks an inaccurate dossier.

Improvements & remediation

  • Re-home or dual-home Delhi metro peering so no single non-compute POP failure can strip regional capacity; validate with node-loss failure testing.
  • Provision N+1 (or better) failover headroom on Delhi-Mumbai and Delhi-Chennai backbones sized to absorb full POP loss, not just marginal load, and monitor headroom continuously.
  • Establish pre-arranged out-of-region peering/backbone burst capacity (e.g. standing Chennai edge capacity) that can be activated in hours rather than provisioned over days.
  • Formalize third-party-facility incident playbooks: contractual re-entry/inspection SLAs, joint safety-clearance procedures, and independent RCA delivery timelines with the operator.
  • Tighten incident-disclosure SLAs so customer-facing notice follows detection closely, with explicit guidance even when no workaround exists.
  • Require battery/UPS energy-storage rooms at third-party facilities to meet recognized fire-safety standards (e.g. NFPA 855 for ESS, UL 9540A thermal-runaway testing, NFPA 76/TIA-942 telecom facility protection) and audit compliance as a hosting prerequisite.
  • Publish a quantified impact and preventive-action COE (affected-project counts, latency deltas, capacity design) to restore tenant confidence and enable independent verification.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism

The initiating event was a fire at a third-party data-center facility hosting a non-compute Google Cloud POP in the Delhi metro. Fire response required an emergency power shutdown of the networking equipment, which is the correct safety action but instantly removed that POP's capacity from the network. Because the POP terminated metro peering and backbone ingress, its de-energization did not merely fail one device, it subtracted a whole node's worth of capacity from the region. Notably, Google's record never confirms what ignited the fire; the popular lithium-battery/UPS narrative is third-party attribution, so the mechanism is verified only from the emergency power-down onward, not at the point of ignition.

Why it cascaded

Losing the POP would have been contained if surviving backbone and peering links carried enough headroom to absorb its traffic. They did not. Google's own remediation, augmenting Delhi backbone capacity, restoring and adding Internet Edge peering, and building out Delhi-Mumbai and Delhi-Chennai link capacity, is the signature of a headroom shortfall: the fixes all added capacity rather than merely rerouting. The result was elevated latency, intermittent packet loss and non-optimal routing that spread across Delhi, Chennai and Mumbai, because traffic that normally ingressed via Delhi was forced onto congested or longer paths. Google states the effect (reduced available capacity) but not the design flaw (single-homing, edge redundancy tier, or provisioned margin), so the deeper cascade cause is inferred, not disclosed.

Recovery trajectory

Recovery was incremental over roughly two weeks and split into two phases. The first phase (through ~17 June) was network-side and did not require the damaged building: Google optimized backbone headroom, restored a portion of Internet Edge peering to cut Delhi latency, and augmented Delhi-Mumbai and Delhi-Chennai link capacity, plus out-of-region Chennai peering for large Indian ISPs. The second, decisive phase was physical: teams obtained safety-cleared access to the fire-damaged site around 22 June and restored further user-facing backbone capacity on 23 June, with the Chennai peering augmentation completing the same day. Resolution was declared on 29 June. Crucially, the gating constraint for full recovery was site re-entry, not network reconvergence, meaning the outage duration was set by fire-safety clearance timelines outside Google's direct control.

Organizational and systemic factors

Two organizational patterns shaped the incident. First, the facility was third-party (attributed to an STT GDC/Tata JV by external reporting), so Google owned the customer relationship and the network response but not fire suppression, power sequencing, or re-entry authority, which lengthened recovery and created an accountability seam. Second, disclosure lagged: the fire began around 5 June but the first customer notice was 9 June, a roughly four-day gap Google never explains, and no workaround was offered across any of the nine updates for the full ~20-day disclosed window. That combination, an opaque onset and prolonged no-workaround status, is what fueled tenant data-loss fears reported in the press, even though those tenant-loss figures are unverified here and asymmetric against the operator's 'majority recovered / not material' posture.

Cross-incident pattern

This incident fits a recurring class of cloud outages where a facilities-layer failure (fire, power, cooling) at a single site propagates into the network layer because edge/peering capacity lacks full-node failover headroom. The tell is the remediation shape: when the fix is 'add capacity elsewhere and wait for physical access' rather than 'reroute instantly,' the underlying system was provisioned near capacity with a single-site dependency. It also illustrates the shared-responsibility blind spot of third-party colocation: hyperscaler resilience narratives assume region-level redundancy, but a non-compute POP concentrated in one leased building can still degrade multiple metros. Finally, it is a provenance cautionary tale, the most-repeated cause (lithium/UPS fire) is not the operator-confirmed cause, underscoring that battery/ESS fire risk is real engineering context but was not a load-bearing, verified fact in this record.

Technical deep-dive

At the engineering level this is a physical-trigger, capacity-exhaustion cascade at the network edge and backbone layer, distinct from a compute-plane fault. A fire at a third-party data center hosting a Google non-compute local POP triggered an emergency power shutdown of networking equipment. De-energizing that equipment removed the POP from the network, losing its Internet Edge peering capacity (the interconnect where user/ISP traffic enters Google's network in the Delhi metro) and its backbone capacity toward other metros.\n\nThe routing layer responded as designed by redistributing traffic across remaining paths. The reported symptom was 'intermittent periods of elevated latency and possible packet loss' for traffic originating from Delhi, Chennai, Mumbai and surrounding areas. Google explicitly states the POP isolation reduced available network capacity in the metro, and every remediation it lists is a capacity action — so the consistent reading is that redistributed demand met alternate peering/backbone links that lacked enough spare headroom for the pre-incident Delhi load plus their own baseline. As link and peering utilization approaches saturation, queues build (raising round-trip latency) and buffer overflow drops packets, matching the observed symptoms. (Note: the specific statement that redistributed demand 'exceeded regional capacity' is an inference from Google's capacity framing and the symptom, not a verbatim quote from the report.)\n\nBecause inter-metro backbone segments (Delhi-Chennai, Delhi-Mumbai) were part of the impaired capacity, congestion was not confined to Delhi; traffic that would normally use Delhi ingress/egress traversed longer or more congested paths through other metros, spreading degradation regionally. Affected products were Hybrid Connectivity, Media CDN, and VPC. (The proposal's 'Global' scope label is not present in the fetched report and is not asserted here.)\n\nThe remediation confirms a capacity problem rather than a control-plane bug: Google's fixes were all capacity actions — restoring Internet Edge peering capacity in Delhi, augmenting out-of-region capacity in Chennai, optimizing backbone headroom, and restoring Delhi-Chennai and Delhi-Mumbai route capacity. These are physical/topological interventions (bringing peering and backbone capacity back online or provisioning it elsewhere), not software rollbacks, which is why recovery was incremental over roughly three weeks and gated by physically accessing the damaged site (access obtained 2026-06-22 after safety clearance; full restoration 2026-06-26). Google does not expose the colocation site's backup-power, generator, or suppression behavior, so the deep dive cannot extend into the site's electrical or fire-suppression engineering; Google's described action is the deliberate protective shutdown of its own networking equipment in response to the fire.
