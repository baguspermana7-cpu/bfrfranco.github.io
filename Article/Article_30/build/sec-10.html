Checklist: connecting an agent to a BMS

When asked to connect an agent to the building management system, specify what the agent may read, what it may write, and what stands between its suggestion and the plant. Government guidance on AI in operational technology (OT) matches the architecture in section 9; the checklist below applies it. Rule: read freely, propose in writing, and let only the existing control system, with its interlocks and a person, act.

10.1 Guidance used

  • CISA, ASD’s ACSC, NSA’s AI Security Center, the FBI and five partner agencies, Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology (published 2025-12-03; PDF revision January 2026). Four principles: understand AI; consider AI use in the OT domain; establish AI governance and assurance frameworks; embed oversight and failsafe practices. It advises to “limit active control of OT infrastructure by AI without a human in the loop to account for safety concerns and latency limitations” and to “regularly update and validate AI models” [S33]. The phrase is human in the loop.
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, released 2023-01-26 [S34]. Its four functions are commonly given as Govern, Map, Measure and Manage; the document was not re-read for this article.
  • ISO/IEC 42001:2023, a management-system standard for AI (ISO/IEC 42001); its scope was not re-read for this article [S35].
  • IEC 62443 for security zones and conduits in industrial systems and IEC 61511 for safety instrumented systems (IEC 62443, SIS); cited as background, not read for this article [S36]. NIST’s framework is covered in the glossary under NIST AI RMF.
  • OWASP LLM01 and the agentic Top 10 for injection and tool risks [S31] [S32].

10.2 Ten-point checklist

  1. Read-only first. The agent reads BMS, EPMS and DCIM trends, alarms and CMMS history. It writes only to a ticket, a draft MOP or a chat message.
  2. No autonomous write to OT, BMS or EPMS. A setpoint, mode or breaker change goes through the existing control system with its interlocks, a human approval and a change record [S33]. Safety functions (SIS, fire, EPO, leak-detection trips) stay out of scope entirely [S36].
  3. One-way data path. The agent sits on the IT side of the DMZ. OT data flows out through a historian replica, and no inbound conduit leads from the agent’s zone to the control zone [S33] [S36].
  4. Typed, allow-listed tools with range checks. A supply-air setpoint limit of, say, 18 to 27 °C is enforced in code, not in the prompt. (The numbers are an illustration, not a recommendation.)
  5. A deterministic verifier per action. A simulator, a rule check or a second data source. For example, confirm a CRAH fault from two independent sensors before raising a priority-one alarm.
  6. Every document, ticket and alarm text is untrusted input. It can carry instructions the agent should not follow [S31] [S32].
  7. Budgets and a kill switch. Limits on steps, cost and wall-clock time, and a manual disable that does not depend on the agent.
  8. Logging and replay. Keep prompts, tool calls and results for incident review, which supports the measure and manage functions in NIST’s framework [S34].
  9. Evaluate before rollout. Use a fixed set of historical incidents with a known correct triage; track precision and recall for each release, and rerun the set when the model or prompt changes.
  10. Codify what repeats. When the agent’s procedure for an alarm class is stable, turn it into a deterministic rule and retire the agent for that class.

Permitted data flow: Trends and alarms move one way from the control zone to a historian replica in the DMZ, and from there to the agent. The agent can draft a work order, and it can send a proposed change with its evidence to an operator. An approved change passes through a change gateway that checks range and interlock conditions before reaching the BMS. No path leads from the agent to the BMS, the PLC or the safety system. The safety system trips equipment independently.

Suitable first tasks: alarm-flood summaries, CMMS drafting, procedure retrieval, shift-handover notes. Common properties: untidy input, a draft output that a person reads, and a wrong answer that costs a minute, not an outage. Out of scope are setpoints, sequences of operation and safety functions. The same rules apply to the agent’s memory: anything it stores between runs is data an attacker could shape, so keep it small, reviewable and expiring.

Summary

“Put an agent on the BMS” becomes “put an agent next to the BMS, with read-only access, a draft-only output, and a person between its suggestion and the setpoint.” Anything beyond that needs its own risk assessment against the guidance [S33].
