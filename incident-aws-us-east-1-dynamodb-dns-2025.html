AWS US-EAST-1 DynamoDB DNS Race Condition Cascades Across the Internet

Amazon Web Services 2025-10-20 14h 32m core impact SoftwareNetwork

A latent race condition in DynamoDB's automated DNS management system left two DNS Enactor processes racing in the us-east-1 region. An unusually delayed Enactor applied an old plan that overwrote a newer one, and a second Enactor's cleanup automation then deleted the plan as stale — instantly removing every IP address for the DynamoDB regional endpoint and leaving clients unable to resolve the service. Because core AWS control planes depend on DynamoDB, the fault cascaded: EC2's DropletWorkflow Manager fell into congestive collapse (insufficient-capacity launch failures), and NLB health-checks began flapping as new instances were brought into service before their network state propagated. DynamoDB DNS was manually corrected by 2:25 AM PDT, but full recovery of dependent services did not complete until 2:20 PM PDT on October 20, 2025 — roughly 14.5 hours after onset. Downdetector logged millions of reports across more than a thousand consumer and enterprise services.

Root cause

The immediate trigger of the October 20, 2025 disruption was a latent race condition in Amazon DynamoDB's automated DNS management system in the US-EAST-1 (Northern Virginia) Region. Per the AWS post-event summary, this race condition resulted in "an incorrect empty DNS record for the service's regional endpoint (dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com)," and the automation was then unable to repair that state. DynamoDB's endpoint DNS is managed by two cooperating automation components: a DNS Planner, which "monitors the health and capacity of the load balancers and periodically creates a new DNS plan," and a DNS Enactor, which applies those plans into Amazon Route53 and which is "designed to have minimal dependencies to allow for system recovery," operating "redundantly and fully independently in three different Availability Zones (AZs)." The mechanism of failure was a timing collision between two independently operating Enactors. Per AWS, "Before it begins to apply a new plan, the DNS Enactor makes a one-time check that its plan is newer than the previously applied plan." On this occasion one Enactor experienced "unusually high delays" while retrying endpoint updates as the Planner produced newer plan generations. Meanwhile a second Enactor rapidly applied the newest plan across all endpoints and then "invoked the plan clean-up process, which identifies plans that are significantly older than the one it just applied and deletes them." The delayed first Enactor then applied "its much older plan to the regional DDB endpoint, overwriting the newer plan" — its one-time freshness check "was stale by this time due to the unusually high delays." When the clean-up process deleted that just-applied older plan, "all IP addresses for the regional endpoint were immediately removed," leaving an empty DNS record. The safeguards did not prevent the outcome. The redundancy design — three fully independent Enactors across three AZs — was intended to survive AZ or host failures, but this was not an infrastructure failure; it was a logical race between the redundant actors themselves, independently mutating shared Route53 state with coordination resting only on a one-time staleness check that could go stale under processing delay, paired with an aggressive clean-up step that deleted the very plan the delayed Enactor had just written. Worse, once the record was emptied, "the system was left in an inconsistent state that prevented subsequent plan updates from being applied by any DNS Enactors," so the automation could not self-heal and manual operator intervention was required. Escalation was immediate and broad. Systems connecting to DynamoDB in us-east-1 via the public endpoint failed DNS resolution and could not connect. The failure cascaded into dependent services. EC2's DropletWorkflow Manager (DWFM), which maintains a lease for each managed droplet, began failing state checks: "Starting at 11:48 PM PDT on October 19, these DWFM state checks began to fail as the process depends on DynamoDB and was unable to complete." Even after DynamoDB DNS was restored, "DWFM had entered a state of congestive collapse and was unable to make forward progress in recovering droplet leases," requiring engineers to throttle incoming work (at 4:14 AM PDT) and perform selective host restarts (leases re-established by 5:28 AM). DWFM recovery then produced "a significant backlog of network state propagations" in Network Manager, delaying connectivity for newly launched EC2 instances. During that window, propagation lag caused NLB health checks to fail against otherwise healthy nodes: per AWS, "health checks would fail even though the underlying NLB node and backend targets were healthy," which "resulted in health checks alternating between failing and healthy" and "caused NLB nodes and backend targets to be removed from DNS, only to be returned to service when the next health check succeeded" — producing further customer-visible errors. Detection was fast: DynamoDB DNS was identified as the source at 12:38 AM PDT (roughly 50 minutes after the 11:48 PM onset); all DNS information was manually restored by 2:25 AM and primary DynamoDB disruption ended by 2:40 AM. The downstream EC2/Network Manager/NLB cascade extended recovery, with all EC2 APIs and new instance launches operating normally by 1:50 PM PDT (13:50).

Contributing factors

  • Concurrency design flaw: multiple DNS Enactors operated 'redundantly and fully independently in three different Availability Zones' and were allowed to modify the same shared Route53 endpoint state concurrently, with coordination resting on a single one-time freshness check rather than an atomic compare-and-swap or lock. This turned an availability safeguard (3-AZ redundancy) into the source of a logical race.
  • Stale-check timing weakness: the Enactor's freshness guard was a 'one-time check' made before it 'begins to apply a new plan,' so 'unusually high delays' in Enactor processing allowed that check to go stale before the write landed, permitting a much older plan to overwrite a newer one.
  • Aggressive clean-up/delete step without safety interlock: the plan clean-up process deletes plans 'significantly older than the one it just applied,' and in this race it deleted the just-applied older plan, so 'all IP addresses for the regional endpoint were immediately removed.' No guard prevented deletion from emptying the active record.
  • No self-healing / inconsistent-state trap: emptying the record left 'the system in an inconsistent state that prevented subsequent plan updates from being applied by any DNS Enactors,' so the automation could not recover on its own and required manual intervention, extending the outage.
  • Deep single-region dependency concentration: control-plane systems such as EC2's DropletWorkflow Manager depend on DynamoDB in us-east-1, so one endpoint DNS failure cascaded into EC2 lease management, Network Manager propagation, and NLB health checking, amplifying a DNS fault into a broad multi-service outage.
  • Congestive-collapse and backlog dynamics in recovery: DWFM entered 'congestive collapse' and could not make forward progress after DynamoDB returned, requiring throttling plus selective restarts; the subsequent Network Manager propagation backlog then made healthy NLB nodes flap in and out of DNS — recovery-time behaviors that lengthened total impact well beyond the initial DNS fix.

Correction of errors (COE)

  • Disable the DynamoDB DNS Planner and DNS Enactor automation worldwide; fix the race-condition scenario and add protections preventing application of incorrect DNS plans before re-enabling. AWS DynamoDB team · Automation disabled (done); race-condition fix and additional protections pending before re-enable
  • Add an additional EC2 test suite that specifically exercises DWFM recovery, so congestive-collapse recovery paths are validated. AWS EC2 team · Committed
  • Introduce an NLB velocity-control mechanism that limits how much capacity can be removed during health-check-driven AZ failover, preventing mass removal from DNS. AWS NLB team · Committed
  • Investigate additional ways to avoid impact from a similar event and to further reduce time to recovery. AWS (broad/organizational) · Committed

Lessons learnt

  • A benign-looking cleanup/garbage-collection step (deleting a 'stale' plan) can be catastrophic when it acts on the currently active record without confirming it is not in use — 'stale by age' is not the same as 'safe to delete.'
  • Automation that mutates shared authoritative state (DNS records) must be serialized or fenced so a delayed actor cannot overwrite a newer plan; last-writer-wins on stale data is a latent time bomb.
  • Deep, implicit dependencies on a single foundational service (DynamoDB) turn a localized fault into a regional cascade; control planes need dependency isolation and graceful degradation.
  • Congestive collapse in a recovery subsystem (DWFM) shows that recovery paths themselves must have back-pressure and be load-tested, not just steady-state operations.
  • Bringing capacity into rotation before its network state has propagated causes health-check flapping; readiness gating must confirm full propagation before a target is advertised in DNS.
  • Regional concentration of critical dependencies (us-east-1) magnifies blast radius; the same single fault reached 1,000+ downstream services because so much depends on one region.

Improvements & remediation

  • Add mutual-exclusion / plan-generation fencing to the DNS Enactor so an older plan can never overwrite a newer applied plan, and cleanup can never delete the active plan.
  • Gate DNS-record deletion on an explicit 'not currently applied' check rather than generation age alone.
  • Build DWFM recovery test coverage and add back-pressure/circuit-breaking so lease recovery degrades gracefully instead of collapsing.
  • Add NLB velocity control to bound capacity removal during health-check AZ failover, and gate target advertisement on confirmed network-state propagation.
  • Reduce cross-service dependency concentration on a single regional endpoint and strengthen recovery automation to shorten time-to-recovery.
  • Improve observability so an empty/incorrect authoritative DNS record is detected and alarmed within seconds of being written, not after downstream failures surface.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism: how an empty DNS record was written

DynamoDB manages the IP addresses behind its regional endpoint through an automated pipeline of DNS Planners (which compute the desired record) and DNS Enactors (which apply plans). The design tolerated two Enactors operating at once. On the night of October 19, one Enactor was unusually delayed and applied a much older plan to the us-east-1 regional endpoint, overwriting the newer, correct plan. The second Enactor's cleanup routine then evaluated that older plan as stale — many generations behind the plan it had just applied — and deleted it. Because deletion removed the record's IP addresses, the endpoint was left with an incorrect empty DNS record. The bug was not in any single component behaving wrongly in isolation; it was the timing interleaving of a delayed apply followed by an age-based cleanup, i.e. a classic latent race condition that had never before lined up.

Why it cascaded: foundational dependency and congestive collapse

DynamoDB is a foundational dependency for AWS control planes, so an unresolvable endpoint did not stay contained. Starting at the same 11:48 PM PDT moment, EC2's DropletWorkflow Manager (DWFM) — which relies on DynamoDB for state — could no longer complete its state checks, and droplet leases began timing out. DWFM then entered congestive collapse: it could not make forward progress recovering leases, so EC2 launch requests returned insufficient-capacity errors. A second, distinct cascade hop hit Network Load Balancer: as recovery brought new EC2 instances online, the health-checking subsystem advertised them before their network state had propagated. Health checks flapped between healthy and failing, and NLB nodes and backend targets were repeatedly pulled from and returned to DNS, producing widespread connection errors. The single DNS fault thus fanned out through two independent failure modes into ECS/EKS/Fargate, Connect, STS, IAM, Redshift and 1,000+ downstream internet services.

Recovery: root fix fast, tail long

The root fault was corrected relatively quickly — engineers manually restored all DNS information for the regional endpoint by 2:25 AM PDT, roughly 2.6 hours after onset. But restoring DNS did not restore the dependent systems, which had accumulated their own backlog and instability. NLB health-check failures were detected by monitoring at 6:52 AM; network configuration propagation times did not return to normal until 10:36 AM; and AWS only began relaxing protective request throttles at 11:23 AM. The overall event did not end until 2:20 PM PDT — about 14.5 hours total. The long tail illustrates that in tightly coupled systems, mean-time-to-recovery is governed less by fixing the trigger than by draining the cascade it set off.

Organizational and systemic factors

Several systemic conditions turned a rare timing bug into a global event. The DNS automation lacked fencing to stop a stale actor from overwriting newer state, and its cleanup deleted a record on age alone without confirming it was inactive. Recovery subsystems like DWFM had no tested back-pressure to prevent congestive collapse. Readiness gating in NLB advertised targets before their network state propagated. And the concentration of critical control-plane dependencies in us-east-1 meant one region's fault propagated widely. AWS's remediation set — disabling the DNS automation worldwide pending a race fix and added plan-validation protections, a DWFM-recovery test suite, and an NLB velocity control — maps directly onto these systemic gaps rather than merely patching the immediate symptom.

Cross-incident pattern: DNS and automation-induced outages

This event fits a recurring industry pattern in which large-scale cloud outages originate not from hardware failure but from automation acting on authoritative shared state — frequently DNS or configuration systems — under a rare timing condition. The trigger is small and self-inflicted (an automated cleanup deleting a record), the blast radius is enormous because foundational services sit beneath everything else, and the visible damage is measured in downstream consumer apps (Snapchat, Reddit, Roblox, Venmo, Alexa, and more) rather than in the originating component. It reinforces the standing lesson that control-plane automation touching global state needs the same rigor — serialization, validation gates, blast-radius limits, and tested recovery — as the data plane it manages.

Technical deep-dive

DynamoDB fronts its regional endpoint (dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com) with load balancers whose membership and weights are published to Route53. Two automation components maintain this mapping. The DNS Planner "monitors the health and capacity of the load balancers and periodically creates a new DNS plan." The DNS Enactor consumes these plans and writes them into Route53; it is "designed to have minimal dependencies to allow for system recovery" and runs as three fully independent instances, one per Availability Zone, so no single AZ or host failure can stop DNS updates. The correctness of this scheme relies on ordering: newer plans must win. The only ordering guard is that, per AWS, "Before it begins to apply a new plan, the DNS Enactor makes a one-time check that its plan is newer than the previously applied plan." This check is not re-validated at commit time, so it is only safe if the interval between check and write is short. On October 19-20, one Enactor suffered unusually high delays retrying endpoint updates while the Planner generated newer plan generations. A second, independent Enactor rapidly applied the newest plan across all endpoints, then invoked "the plan clean-up process, which identifies plans that are significantly older than the one it just applied and deletes them." The delayed first Enactor then finally applied "its much older plan to the regional DDB endpoint, overwriting the newer plan" — its freshness check having gone stale. The clean-up logic then deleted that just-applied older plan, and "all IP addresses for the regional endpoint were immediately removed," producing an empty answer set. From that instant every client resolving the public endpoint failed to connect. The empty-record end state was also a trap for the automation: "the system was left in an inconsistent state that prevented subsequent plan updates from being applied by any DNS Enactors," so the Planner/Enactor pipeline could not simply publish a corrected plan over the damage. Operators intervened manually; DynamoDB DNS was identified as the source at 12:38 AM PDT, all DNS information was restored by 2:25 AM, global table replicas caught up by 2:32 AM, and primary DynamoDB disruption ended by 2:40 AM. The cascade illustrates control-plane dependency coupling. EC2's DropletWorkflow Manager (DWFM) maintains a lease for each managed droplet and must complete a periodic state check with each; per AWS, "Starting at 11:48 PM PDT on October 19, these DWFM state checks began to fail as the process depends on DynamoDB and was unable to complete," and leases timed out between 11:48 PM and 2:24 AM. After DynamoDB recovered, DWFM was in "congestive collapse" — pending lease-recovery work exceeded throughput and no forward progress occurred — so engineers throttled incoming work at 4:14 AM and selectively restarted DWFM hosts, re-establishing all leases by 5:28 AM. That recovery generated a large Network Manager backlog of network-state propagations (elevated latencies noted by 6:21 AM), delaying connectivity for newly launched instances until propagation normalized around 10:36 AM. During this window propagation lag made NLB health checks intermittently fail against healthy nodes: "health checks would fail even though the underlying NLB node and backend targets were healthy," which "resulted in health checks alternating between failing and healthy" and "caused NLB nodes and backend targets to be removed from DNS, only to be returned to service when the next health check succeeded." EC2 APIs and new instance launches were operating normally by 1:50 PM PDT (13:50). AWS's corrective actions map onto the mechanism: it "disabled the DynamoDB DNS Planner and the DNS Enactor automation worldwide," committed to "fix the race condition scenario and add additional protections to prevent the application of incorrect DNS plans" before re-enabling, is "adding a velocity control mechanism to limit the capacity a single NLB can remove when health check failures cause AZ failover," is building an additional scale test suite to exercise the DWFM recovery workflow, and is improving throttling in EC2 data-propagation systems to rate-limit incoming work based on the size of the waiting queue.
