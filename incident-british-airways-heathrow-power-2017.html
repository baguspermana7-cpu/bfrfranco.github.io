British Airways Boadicea House Power Surge: A 15-Minute Fault That Grounded Heathrow and Gatwick for Days

British Airways (IAG); data-centre facility management by CBRE 2017-05-27 48h 0m core impact PowerHuman error

On the morning of Saturday 27 May 2017, an engineer working inside British Airways' Boadicea House data centre in West London disconnected a power supply and then reconnected it in an uncontrolled, unplanned manner. The resulting surge physically damaged servers and bypassed the site's backup generators and batteries. Although the BoHo site itself was without power for only about 15 minutes, the physical damage plus a failed failover to the secondary data centre escalated a brief electrical fault into a multi-day service outage. British Airways grounded all flights at Heathrow and Gatwick, affecting roughly 75,000 passengers and as many as 672 flights. IAG later confirmed a loss of about £58m. No formal operator postmortem was ever published; BA sued its facilities supplier CBRE and the dispute settled in February 2019 with no admission of liability.

Root cause

No authoritative or adjudicated root cause was ever published for the 27 May 2017 British Airways outage at the Boadicea House (BoHo) core data centre near London Heathrow. Every root-cause account on record originates from the operator side (IAG/BA), was disputed by the facilities manager (CBRE), and the litigation that would have tested the competing accounts was resolved without any published judicial finding on cause. The reconstruction below reflects the best-corroborated operator account, with disputed and unverified points flagged. TRIGGER (human/operational). Per the leaked internal explanation attributed to IAG Group IT head Bill Francis, an Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) feeding the BoHo core data centre was "over-ridden" on the Saturday morning, which "resulted in the total immediate loss of power to the facility, bypassing the backup generators and batteries" (The Register; the same "bypassing the backup generators and batteries" line appears in the leaked IAG email reported by Network World). IAG CEO Willie Walsh framed the same trigger in human terms - an engineer disconnected a power supply - which BA later characterised in its High Court claim as an "unauthorised reconnection of servers after they had been accidentally unplugged from the power supply by an engineer" (ch-aviation). The two operator descriptions are consistent in outcome: an immediate, total loss of power to the facility. WHY THE SAFEGUARDS DID NOT CARRY THE LOAD. The defining feature is that the redundant power infrastructure did not carry the load: the internal account states the loss occurred while "bypassing the backup generators and batteries." Rather than transferring seamlessly to battery-backed UPS output and generator power, the facility went fully dark. Contemporary reporting indicated the external grid supply was not the cause and the fault lay inside the facility's own power chain; however, the specific National Grid / SSE statements sometimes cited for this point could not be independently re-verified in this review and should be treated as reported, not confirmed. PROPAGATION - THE DAMAGE-CAUSING EVENT. The outage itself did not destroy the hardware; the uncontrolled RESTORATION did. "After a few minutes of this shutdown of power, it was turned back on in an unplanned and uncontrolled fashion, which created physical damage to the system" (The Register). A BA spokesperson restated the mechanism: "There was a loss of power to the U.K. data center, which was compounded by the uncontrolled return of power, which caused a power surge taking out our IT systems" (Network World). The causal weight sits on "uncontrolled": restoring power to a large IT load without proper sequencing produced a surge that physically damaged servers and IT hardware. One unattributed technical scenario in The Register speculated that the battery and generator supplies were briefly connected in series, yielding roughly 480V instead of 240V and causing a "literal meltdown" - but this is explicitly speculation from an anonymous source and is NOT officially confirmed. ESCALATION AND RECOVERY. Because the failure physically damaged hardware rather than merely interrupting it, recovery required repair and rebuild, extending the event into a multi-day, airline-wide disruption. The impact stranded roughly 75,000 passengers and forced about 672 flight cancellations; IAG put the cost at around EUR65 million (about USD74 million) per ch-aviation (higher £80m-plus figures cited elsewhere could not be verified here). DISPUTED / UNRESOLVED STATUS. CBRE, the facilities manager, disputed the engineer-error account, stating at the time that "no determination has been made yet regarding the causes of the incident on May 27" (Network World). BA commissioned an independent audit whose findings were never published, and filed a claim against CBRE in the London High Court (ch-aviation). The suit was subsequently resolved; the specific settlement date and any "no admission of liability" wording could not be verified from approved sources, but no judicial ruling on cause was ever published. Accordingly, the human/operational trigger and the uncontrolled-restoration damage mechanism are the operator's account - corroborated across multiple operator statements and a leaked internal email - but never independently adjudicated.

Contributing factors

  • Power-chain design / failover weakness: overriding a single UPS produced a 'total immediate loss of power to the facility, bypassing the backup generators and batteries' - the redundant generator and battery infrastructure did not automatically carry the load, turning one action into a full facility blackout (The Register; leaked IAG email via Network World).
  • Lack of a controlled power-restoration procedure: power was 'turned back on in an unplanned and uncontrolled fashion,' with no enforced staged / sequenced re-energisation to limit inrush and surge - which converted a recoverable outage into physical hardware destruction (The Register; Network World).
  • Human/operational error and change-control gap: the trigger is attributed to an engineer disconnecting a power supply and an 'unauthorised reconnection,' pointing to weaknesses in authorisation, procedure and supervision around live power work in a core data centre (ch-aviation; Network World).
  • Split operational responsibility between BA (IAG) and outsourced facilities manager CBRE: the two parties gave conflicting accounts and disputed responsibility, and the cause was never jointly determined - an organisational/governance factor that left root cause unresolved (Network World).
  • No power-side protection preventing an internal transient from destroying IT hardware: because servers could be physically damaged by a single surge event, recovery required rebuilding damaged systems rather than a simple failover or restart (Network World; The Register).
  • Absence of a published, authoritative post-incident investigation: BA's commissioned independent audit was never published and the High Court litigation produced no published ruling on cause, so no corrective root cause was ever formally established or shared (ch-aviation).

Lessons learnt

  • A brief physical fault can become a multi-day outage. The BoHo site lost power for only ~15 minutes, yet recovery took days because the surge caused physical hardware damage and the failover was defeated — time-to-recover is governed by damage and data integrity, not by fault duration.
  • Redundancy that shares a failure path is not redundancy. An active/active pair that synchronises corrupted state during an uncommanded shutdown can propagate the fault to the backup, turning a single-site incident into a total service loss.
  • Human error during restoration is as dangerous as during the initial fault. Walsh's own framing — that it is easy to understand a mistake in disconnecting power but hard to understand a mistake in reconnecting it — highlights that the reconnection step needs the same rigour, gating and supervision as any hazardous change.
  • Authorisation to be present is not authorisation to act. Physical access and permitted-action scope must be separated and enforced procedurally, not left to individual judgement.
  • Absence of a published, agreed root cause harms industry learning. The settlement with no admission of liability and no jointly agreed cause leaves the sector to learn from leaked emails and press reconstruction rather than a validated RCA.

Improvements & remediation

  • Enforce change management on power operations: any disconnect/reconnect of live UPS/generator/battery supplies should require a permit-to-work, a second-person verification, and a controlled, ramped re-energisation sequence that cannot bridge battery and generator sources onto the rack bus.
  • Harden failover against fault propagation: add integrity gating and quorum/health checks before synchronising state to the secondary site, so an uncommanded shutdown cannot replicate corrupted data and crash the standby.
  • Separate physical access from action authorisation, with technical interlocks and supervision for hazardous switching, so being authorised to enter the room never implies being authorised to operate power equipment.
  • Rehearse damage-mode recovery, not just power-loss recovery: run drills that assume physical hardware damage and corrupted replication, with validated data-restore and reconciliation procedures to prevent stale-data symptoms during recovery.
  • Commit to publishing (or independently reviewing) post-incident RCAs for critical-national-infrastructure-scale outages so root cause and corrective actions are transparent and auditable.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism: a human error at the power boundary

The initiating fault was not equipment failure but human mis-operation. On the Saturday morning of 27 May 2017, a power supply at Boadicea House was disconnected and then reconnected in an uncontrolled, unplanned manner. The reconnection surge physically damaged servers and bypassed the backup generators and batteries entirely. This account is dual-sourced — a leaked IAG internal email carried by the BBC and Willie Walsh on the record — and it resolves the early 'defective UPS' versus 'working perfectly' contradiction: the equipment did its job; a person operating it did not. A single technical outlet (The Register) offers a plausible physical scenario in which the restoration briefly connected battery and generator supply in series to the rack bus, feeding servers 480V instead of 240V and causing a literal meltdown. This remains a hypothesis consistent with the confirmed physical damage rather than an established fact.

Why it cascaded: redundancy defeated by shared state

The most consequential aspect is that BoHo itself was down for only about a quarter of an hour, yet service was lost for days. The bridge from a 15-minute fault to a multi-day outage runs through two amplifiers. First, the surge caused physical hardware damage, so recovery required hardware remediation, not a restart. Second, the failover did not save the service. The leading hypothesis is that the uncommanded shutdown synchronised corrupted data from BoHo into the secondary data centre, populating it with bad data and crashing the dependent systems — a shared-fault mode in which the backup inherited the primary's corruption. The Sunday 28 May symptom of customers seeing stale booking data offers weak independent corroboration that data integrity and replication were affected.

Recovery and impact: a dual-hub grounding

Because the failover could not carry the load and hardware was physically damaged, British Airways grounded all flights at Heathrow and Gatwick. On the Saturday, BA initially cancelled flights until 18:00 BST and then extended the window; an aviation expert forecast three to four days of disruption. As many as 672 flights were grounded and roughly 75,000 passengers affected — the passenger figure cross-checked across DCD's settlement reporting and the IAG/BBC account, while the specific flight count rests on a single outlet. Recovery took on the order of two to three days, with operations normalising around Monday 29 May. IAG later confirmed the financial impact at about £58m (~$74.6m), covering passenger compensation (under the EU261 schedule of EUR250/EUR400/up to EUR600) and lost business; higher press figures of $100m and up to £150m were never reconciled to an audited total.

Organisational and systemic factors

The incident exposed governance gaps as much as engineering ones. The engineer was authorised to be in the data centre but not authorised to do what he did, indicating that physical access and permitted-action scope were not separated or interlocked. There was no gate to stop an uncontrolled reconnection of a hazardous power source. The blame allocation was never technically settled: BA sued facilities supplier CBRE roughly 18 months later, and the dispute settled on 11 February 2019 with no admission of liability, an undisclosed amount, and no jointly agreed root cause. Critically, no formal operator or regulatory postmortem was ever published, so the entire mechanism narrative rests on press attribution and a leaked internal email — a transparency deficit for an outage of national significance.

Cross-incident pattern: the small-fault, large-blast-radius archetype

This event is a canonical example of a category where a minor, brief, human-triggered fault produces an outsized outage because designed protections are bypassed and redundancy shares a failure path. The signature elements — human error during a maintenance/restoration action, backup power bypassed, and a failover that inherits corruption rather than isolating it — recur across major data-centre incidents. The lesson generalises: resilience is determined less by the redundancy that exists on paper than by whether the failure modes of the primary and backup are genuinely independent, and by whether hazardous human actions at the power boundary are procedurally gated. Here, both assumptions broke at once.

Technical deep-dive

The failure mechanism, as described by the operator, is a two-stage electrical event centred on the facility's UPS/backup power chain, with the second stage causing the actual hardware damage. All of it is the operator (IAG/BA) account plus a leaked internal email; CBRE disputed it and no independent adjudication confirmed it. Stage 1 - loss of source without failover. A UPS feeding the BoHo core data centre was "over-ridden" (Walsh's version: an engineer disconnected a power supply). In a properly configured N+1/2N data-centre topology, removing one source should cause an automatic transfer so the load continues on battery-backed UPS output and, for a sustained outage, on generator power. Instead the event "resulted in the total immediate loss of power to the facility, bypassing the backup generators and batteries" - the batteries and generators did not pick up the load and the facility de-energised immediately (The Register; leaked IAG email via Network World). Contemporary reporting placed the fault inside the facility's own distribution rather than upstream on the grid, but the specific National Grid / SSE 'customer side of the meter' statements could not be re-verified in this review and are treated as reported, not confirmed. Stage 2 - uncontrolled re-energisation and surge (the damaging event). 'After a few minutes... it was turned back on in an unplanned and uncontrolled fashion, which created physical damage to the system,' and the 'uncontrolled return of power... caused a power surge taking out our IT systems.' Engineering-wise, re-applying power to a large, fully-de-energised IT load without sequencing produces a severe inrush transient - many power supplies simultaneously drawing charging/magnetising current, no staged ramp, and a potentially unstable source still stabilising. Without soft-start, staged breaker closing, inrush limiting, or an orderly transfer back onto conditioned UPS output, the resulting voltage/current transient can exceed the ratings of server PSUs and other IT hardware, producing physical rather than merely logical damage. One unattributed scenario in The Register speculated the battery and generator supplies were briefly connected in series, delivering roughly 480V instead of 240V and causing a 'literal meltdown'; this is explicitly speculation from an anonymous source, never officially confirmed. Consequence for recovery. Because the damage was physical, systems could not simply be powered back up - damaged hardware had to be repaired/replaced and systems rebuilt, consistent with the multi-day, airline-wide disruption (about 672 cancellations, roughly 75,000 passengers stranded). None of these electrical specifics were ever independently adjudicated.
