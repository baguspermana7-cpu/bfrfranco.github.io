NIRS Daejeon Government Data Center: UPS Lithium-Ion Battery Fire During Relocation Work

National Information Resources Service (NIRS) 2025-09-26 10h 0m core impact FirePower

On the evening of 26 September 2025, a lithium-ion UPS battery that had been disconnected in order to be relocated to the basement of the NIRS Daejeon government data center entered thermal runaway and exploded in a server room, igniting a fire that took roughly ten hours to extinguish. When the fire broke out, all 647 government IT systems at the Daejeon headquarters were taken offline (96 of them directly destroyed, the rest shut down as a precautionary safety measure). Core national services including digital IDs, Korea Post postal banking, emergency GPS tracking, courts and tax were paralyzed, and all documents on the G-Drive cloud used by up to 30 ministries and nearly 191,000 employees were permanently lost because the repository was not backed up externally. Recovery reached 70.5 percent by 24 October and later exceeded 95 percent; the NIRS chief and three other officials were booked for causing the fire through on-duty negligence, the chief was relieved of duty, and Daejeon police raided NIRS.

Root cause

The ignition source was an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) lithium-ion battery that had been disconnected in order to be relocated to the basement of the facility. The battery entered thermal runaway and exploded inside a server room, igniting the fire. Two independent primary reports confirm the causal chain: relocation work on a disconnected lithium-ion UPS battery, which underwent a thermal-runaway explosion that started the server-room fire. Officials were booked for causing the fire through on-duty negligence, framing the legal theory as negligent (not intentional) causation tied to the battery-move work.

Contributing factors

  • Lithium-ion UPS batteries were being physically relocated while in a state that permitted thermal runaway, with the ignition occurring inside a live server room rather than an isolated, fire-rated battery area.
  • Co-location and precautionary shutdown: when the fire broke out, all 647 co-located systems at the Daejeon headquarters were taken offline simultaneously as a safety measure, so the operational impact extended far beyond the 96 systems physically destroyed.
  • Absence of a recoverable backup for the G-Drive cloud repository: the system was not backed up externally, so once destroyed all documents for up to 30 ministries were lost with no way to bring them back, indicating no effective off-site or isolated copy.
  • Concentration of critical national services (digital ID, postal banking, courts, tax, emergency GPS) in a single facility, so one physical event cascaded into simultaneous nationwide service paralysis.
  • Battery-relocation maintenance performed adjacent to production server infrastructure, exposing running systems to an energetic failure of the component being moved.

Correction of errors (COE)

  • Establish and enforce a controlled procedure for relocating or decommissioning lithium-ion UPS batteries — including verified de-energization, state-of-charge management, thermal monitoring, isolation from live server rooms, and a hot-work / hazardous-work permit — so a battery move can never expose production infrastructure to thermal runaway. Lessons learnt

  • Lithium-ion UPS batteries are an energetic hazard throughout their life, including during disconnection and relocation — the failure here occurred not in normal operation but during a battery move, so maintenance and decommissioning work must be treated as a high-risk activity with dedicated safeguards.
  • Physical proximity of energy-storage components to production servers converts a localized battery event into direct destruction of IT systems; battery installations belong in segregated, fire-rated rooms, not in or beside server halls.
  • A precautionary full shutdown of a co-located estate turns a single-room fire into a facility-wide outage: all 647 systems were taken offline for safety, far beyond the 96 physically destroyed, so concentrating many critical systems in one facility magnifies the operational blast radius (647 systems, 709 services).
  • A cloud repository without a recoverable, isolated backup is a single point of catastrophic data loss — because G-Drive was not backed up externally, the permanent loss of all its documents for up to 30 ministries shows that 'cloud' alone does not equal durable protection.
  • Concentrating critical national services in one facility creates systemic fragility; digital ID, postal banking, courts, tax and emergency GPS all failing at once demonstrates the need for geographic and architectural diversity.
  • A clear restoration priority order (cooling, then sequential server restart, essential services first, Grade-1 tracked) materially aids recovery and public communication, and should be pre-defined rather than improvised.

Improvements & remediation

  • Mandate hazardous-work permits, verified de-energization, thermal monitoring and physical isolation for all lithium-ion battery relocation and decommissioning, removing live server rooms from the work zone.
  • Relocate lithium-ion UPS energy storage into dedicated fire-rated battery rooms with early-warning gas/thermal detection and battery-appropriate suppression, separated from IT space.
  • Provision isolated, tested, recoverable backups and cross-site replication for shared government cloud repositories so no single fire can cause permanent multi-ministry data loss.
  • Distribute and geo-replicate critical national services across multiple facilities/availability zones to eliminate single-facility, single-event nationwide paralysis.
  • Compartmentalize and fault-isolate the facility (fire zoning, independent power and cooling per hall) so that a fire in one room does not require a precautionary shutdown of the entire co-located estate.
  • Institutionalize a pre-defined, drilled restoration runbook with priority tiers (Grade-1 services, cooling-first sequencing) and transparent progress reporting.

Comprehensive analysis

Failure mechanism: thermal runaway during battery relocation

The incident originated not in steady-state operation but in maintenance activity. A lithium-ion UPS battery was disconnected to be relocated to the basement, and during or after that handling it entered thermal runaway and exploded inside a server room. Two independent primary reports converge on this mechanism — the disconnected UPS lithium-ion battery as ignition source, and the explosion of lithium-ion batteries in a server room as the cited main cause. The energetic, self-sustaining nature of lithium-ion thermal runaway meant a single pack could ignite a fire that ultimately required 101 firefighters, 31 vehicles, and roughly ten hours to extinguish.

Why the outage reached 647 systems

The physical fire was localized, yet its operational impact was national through two distinct paths. First, the battery failure occurred inside a live server room, so the explosion could directly destroy IT hardware — 96 of 647 systems were confirmed not merely offline but destroyed. Second, when the fire broke out all 647 co-located systems at the Daejeon headquarters were taken offline simultaneously as a preemptive safety measure, not because untouched rooms were technically dependent on the burned ones. The combined result was 647 systems offline and 709 services disrupted, sweeping in digital IDs, Korea Post postal banking and delivery, emergency GPS tracking, courts and tax — an operational blast radius set by the concentration of systems in one facility and the precautionary shutdown, well beyond the physical fire footprint.

Permanent data loss and the backup gap

The most durable damage was informational. The G-Drive cloud held work files for up to 30 ministries and had been allotted to nearly 191,000 employees (of 750,000 public-sector officials nationwide) as of August 2025. Officials confirmed the system was not backed up externally, so all documents on the repository were lost with no way to recover them, making this a permanent loss rather than an outage. The distribution of harm tracked reliance on the cloud: the Ministry of Personnel Management, which had concentrated most of its work on G-Drive since a 2016 office break-in, was hardest hit, while the Office for Government Policy Coordination, which did not rely heavily on the cloud, was least affected. The absence of any recoverable copy is the clearest systemic failure exposed by the fire.

Recovery trajectory and prioritization

Restoration was staged and measurable. The ministry prioritized bringing back the cooling system first, then sequentially restarted servers, restoring essential services such as postal banking and delivery ahead of others. By 6 p.m. on 24 October the restoration rate reached 70.5 percent — 500 of 709 services online, 34 of 40 Grade-1 priority services restored, and the Document 24 online document service normalized. By the time of the negligence-booking report, more than 95 percent of services had been restored. The disciplined priority order (cooling, sequential restart, essentials first) is a replicable positive.

Organizational, legal and systemic factors

Accountability was pursued as negligence, not intent: NIRS chief Lee Jae-yong and three other officials were booked for causing the fire through on-duty negligence, Lee was relieved of duty, and the Daejeon Metropolitan Police Agency raided NIRS headquarters and searched three firms tied to the UPS system. The framing points to a procedural and governance failure around hazardous battery-relocation work performed adjacent to critical production systems. The deeper systemic issue is concentration risk — a single government facility hosting digital ID, postal banking, courts, tax and emergency services created a fragile monoculture in which one physical event produced nationwide paralysis and irrecoverable data loss.

Cross-incident pattern: lithium-ion energy storage as a rising data-center fire vector

This event fits a broader pattern of lithium-ion battery energy storage emerging as a leading data-center fire and destruction risk, distinct from legacy power or cooling failures. It occurred amid heightened fire concern in the same city — it was reported as the second data center in a week to catch fire in Daejeon. The recurring lessons — segregate batteries from IT space, treat battery maintenance as high-risk, and never depend on a single un-backed repository — generalize well beyond this facility.

Technical deep-dive

The proximate technical event was a thermal-runaway explosion of a lithium-ion UPS battery pack that had been disconnected for relocation to the basement, occurring inside a live server room. Lithium-ion thermal runaway is a self-sustaining exothermic process: once a cell is driven into runaway (by mechanical, thermal or electrical abuse — all plausible during a physical battery move), internal temperatures climb rapidly, the cell vents flammable electrolyte and gases, and heat propagates cell-to-cell through the pack, producing an energetic fire that is difficult to arrest and prone to sustained burning. That the pack exploded in the server room rather than an isolated battery area is the pivotal engineering failure: it placed the ignition energy directly among IT hardware, which is why 96 of 647 systems were physically destroyed rather than merely de-energized. The response profile — 101 firefighters, 31 vehicles, and roughly 10 hours from the 20:20 report to the 06:30 declaration of extinguishment — is consistent with the difficulty of suppressing an energized lithium-ion fire; responders relied on carbon-dioxide gaseous suppression and deliberately minimized water to avoid damaging national data, a choice that further slowed extinguishment. Crucially, the outage's breadth was set by facility policy rather than flame spread: the remaining systems were not physically damaged but were taken offline as a precautionary safety shutdown of the co-located estate, so the operational blast radius (647 systems, 709 services) reflects co-location plus shutdown policy, not an engineered dependency cascade. On the data plane, the G-Drive repository (up to 30 ministries, ~191,000 employees with access) was rendered permanently unrecoverable because it was not backed up externally, implying no isolated, restorable backup or off-site replica existed for that store. Recovery was executed as a controlled cold-start: cooling infrastructure restored first (a prerequisite for safe server operation), then sequential server restart with essential services (postal banking, delivery) prioritized and Grade-1 services tracked to a measurable 70.5 percent at Oct 24 and >95 percent thereafter. Engineering specifics that would refine this analysis — exact cell chemistry (LFP vs NMC), pack format, state-of-charge at disconnection, detection type, NFPA 855 / UL 9540A applicability, and whether the pack reignited — are not disclosed in reachable primary sources and are not asserted here; they represent standard inference points, not incident-specific findings, and do not alter the confirmed root cause.
